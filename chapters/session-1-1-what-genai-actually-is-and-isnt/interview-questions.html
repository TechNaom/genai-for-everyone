Session 1.1 Interview Prep 8 min read

Interview Questions: Core GenAI Concepts

These mirror how real entry-level and early-career GenAI interviews actually open in 2026: fundamentals first, but probing for genuine understanding rather than memorized definitions. Interviewers consistently say they're listening for trade-off reasoning and concrete examples, not textbook recall. Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 Explain the difference between predictive and generative AI, using an example from everyday life.
Strong answer

A clear definition of each — "predictive selects from existing options, generative creates new content" — with one concrete everyday example of each (e.g. a spam filter sorting mail into fixed buckets vs. ChatGPT drafting a fresh email). Ideally a note that real products sometimes combine both.

Red flags

Treating "AI" as one undifferentiated thing, or giving definitions with no concrete example to anchor them.

Follow-up

"Give me a product that uses both at once." (e.g. a support tool that predicts a ticket's category and then generates a suggested reply.)

2 What is a token, and why does the same sentence sometimes cost more in one language than another?
Strong answer

Tokens are sub-word units — not letters, and not always whole words. Common English words are often a single token, while rare words or less-common languages get split into more pieces, so the same meaning can take more tokens in one language than another. This links directly to per-token API pricing.

Red flags

Saying a token is always one word or one character, or not connecting token count to cost.

Follow-up

"If a prompt is getting expensive, name one thing you could do about the token count." (e.g. trim redundant context, or use a more token-efficient phrasing.)

3 Explain embeddings to a non-technical stakeholder, without using any math.
Strong answer

An analogy such as a "map of meaning" works well: the key idea is that similar-meaning words land close together on the map. Ideally the king/queen example to show the relationship is learned from data, not hand-programmed.

Red flags

Diving into vectors and cosine similarity for a non-technical audience, or implying the relationships were manually coded in.

Follow-up

"Why is that 'map of meaning' useful in a product?" (e.g. finding related documents or answers even when the exact words differ.)

4 What is a context window, and what's a practical problem that can arise from a very long conversation?
Strong answer

The "working memory" framing: the context window is what the model can currently see, and anything outside it is invisible to the model. A real consequence — early conversation details get "forgotten," or the "lost in the middle" effect where information buried in the middle of a long document is used less reliably.

Red flags

Believing the model permanently remembers everything it's ever seen, or confusing the context window with training data.

Follow-up

"How might you keep important details available in a very long chat?" (e.g. re-supply or summarize the key facts so they stay inside the window.)

5 Why can a large language model produce a confident, fluent, completely incorrect answer to a factual question?
Strong answer

The core mechanism: the model predicts plausible next tokens, it doesn't retrieve verified facts — so a fluent-but-false continuation and a fluent-but-true one look identical to it. The practical takeaway is that fluency and accuracy are separate properties. This is the seed explanation for hallucination.

Red flags

Saying the model "lied," or assuming a confident tone means the answer was fact-checked somewhere.

Follow-up

"What would you add to a system to reduce this risk?" (e.g. grounding answers in retrieved documents — the seed of RAG, covered later.)

Strategy Tips

  • Cover the "Strong answer" line, answer out loud, then check yourself against it — that's how these are meant to be used for self-testing.
  • Interviewers are listening for trade-off reasoning and concrete examples, not textbook recall. When in doubt, anchor every definition to a real product you can name.
  • It's fine to think out loud ("predictive picks from a fixed set, so a fraud score is predictive...") — the reasoning is what's being evaluated, not just the final word.
