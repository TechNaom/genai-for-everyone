Section A Interview Prep 8 min read

Interview Questions: Core Concepts (Sessions 1.1–1.2)

These mirror how real entry-level GenAI interviews actually open in 2026 — fundamentals first, but probing for genuine understanding rather than memorized definitions. Interviewers listen for trade-off reasoning and concrete examples, not textbook recall.

1 Explain the difference between predictive and generative AI, using an example from everyday life.
Strong answer

Predictive AI selects from existing options or scores them — e.g. your email deciding whether an incoming message is spam or not. Generative AI creates new content that didn't exist before — e.g. ChatGPT drafting the reply to that email. A strong answer gives one concrete example of each and notes that real products often combine both (spam filter + smart-compose in the same inbox).

Red flags

Treating "AI" as one monolithic thing, or claiming generative AI is just "smarter" predictive AI — they answer fundamentally different kinds of questions (choose vs. create).

Follow-up

"Which one would you reach for to flag fraudulent transactions?" (Answer: predictive — it's a classification/scoring problem, not a content-creation one.)

2 What is a token, and why does the same sentence sometimes cost more in one language than another?
Strong answer

A token is the sub-word unit an LLM actually processes — not a letter, and not reliably a whole word. Common English words are often a single token, while rare words and many non-English languages get split into more, smaller pieces. Because APIs bill per token, the same idea expressed in Hindi or Japanese can use more tokens than the English equivalent and therefore cost more.

Red flags

Saying "a token is a word" with no caveat, or assuming cost is about sentence length in characters rather than token count.

Follow-up

"Why does an LLM struggle to count the letters in 'strawberry'?" (Answer: it never sees individual letters — it sees tokens like straw and berry.)

3 Explain embeddings to a non-technical stakeholder, without using any math.
Strong answer

An embedding is a "map of meaning." Instead of plotting cities by location, you plot words by what they mean, so similar-meaning words land close together — "dog" and "puppy" are neighbors; "dog" and "astronomy" are far apart. The clincher is the king − man + woman ≈ queen example: that relationship was learned from how words get used, never explicitly programmed.

Red flags

Reaching for vectors, cosine similarity, or dimensions when the whole point was a non-technical explanation — or implying a human hand-coded which words are "similar."

Follow-up

"Where would you actually use embeddings in a product?" (Answer: retrieval / RAG — finding the document chunks closest in meaning to a question.)

4 What is a context window, and what practical problem can arise from a very long conversation?
Strong answer

The context window is the model's working memory — the maximum amount of text (measured in tokens) it can "see" at once. Anything outside it is simply invisible to the model. In a long conversation, the earliest messages eventually fall off the desk and get "forgotten," and even within the window, information buried in the middle can get less effective attention than text near the edges (the "lost in the middle" effect).

Red flags

Describing "forgetting" as a bug or human-style bad memory, or assuming a bigger context window automatically means better answers.

Follow-up

"A user says the bot forgot what they told it earlier — is that a bug?" (Answer: usually not — it's a context-window limit, not a defect.)

5 Why can a large language model produce a confident, fluent, completely incorrect answer to a factual question?
Strong answer

Because the model predicts the most plausible next token given everything in its context — it isn't retrieving verified facts from a database. Fluency and accuracy are separate properties: the same mechanism that makes text sound natural will happily produce a smooth, confident sentence that happens to be false. That's the seed explanation for hallucination.

Red flags

Calling it "a glitch" or "the model lying," or assuming more training data alone fully eliminates the risk.

Follow-up

"So how do teams reduce it in practice?" (Answer: grounding in retrieved documents, evaluation, and guardrails — covered in later weeks.)
