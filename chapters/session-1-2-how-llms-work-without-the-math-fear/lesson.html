How LLMs Work, Without the Math Fear

Session 1.1 established the what: an LLM predicts the next most plausible piece of text, one piece at a time. This session is the how — and we're doing it without a single equation.

On this page 1. Tokens — the unit an LLM actually reads 2. Embeddings — turning words into math 3. Context windows — the model's working memory 4. Putting the three pieces together
Here's a promise: by the end of this session you'll understand what a "token" is, what an "embedding" is, and what a "context window" is — three words thrown around constantly in GenAI conversations, often by people who half-understand them. You'll understand each well enough to explain it with a clean analogy, and well enough to see why certain things you'll hit later in this program happen the way they do: counting tokens for cost, running into context limits, weird tokenization with non-English text. This is also the session where a few "huh, that's weird" moments finally click — like why ChatGPT sometimes struggles to count the letters in a word. By the end, you'll know exactly why, and it has nothing to do with the model being "bad at counting."
Sub-topic 1 of 4

1. Tokens — the actual unit an LLM "reads"

Most people assume an LLM reads text the way we do — word by word, or even letter by letter. It doesn't. An LLM breaks text into tokens: chunks that are sometimes a whole word, sometimes part of a word, and sometimes just punctuation.

What is... a token? A token is the smallest chunk of text an LLM actually processes. It is not reliably a word and not a letter — it's a sub-word unit. Common words are often one token; rare words get split into several.

Think LEGO bricks, not a smooth clay sculpture. You don't have an infinite variety of uniquely-shaped pieces — you have a fixed set of brick shapes, and you build everything by snapping the right sequence together. That's tokenization: the model has a fixed vocabulary of around 100,000 possible tokens, and every piece of text is assembled out of those bricks.

Illustrative tokenization 
"the cat sat"                    ->  the | cat | sat                              (3 tokens)
"unbelievable"                   ->  un | believ | able                          (3 tokens)
"GenAI"                          ->  Gen | AI                                     (2 tokens)
"antidisestablishmentarianism"  ->  ant | idis | establishment | arian | ism     (5 tokens)
"🎉"                             ->  a single emoji token (sometimes 2-3)         (1-3 tokens)

Notice the pattern: common, everyday words tend to be single tokens because they appeared so frequently in training that the tokenizer "learned" to treat them as one unit. Rare or made-up words get chopped into smaller, more common sub-pieces.

What is... why non-English text costs more? If the tokenizer's vocabulary was built mostly from English text, languages like Hindi, Japanese, or Arabic get split into smaller, less efficient pieces — so the same idea uses more tokens. Since you're billed per token, the same sentence can cost meaningfully more in one language than another. This has real consequences in Week 6 (Cost & Latency Engineering).

You don't have to take this on faith — you can watch it happen. Here's the core of this session's exercise, using tiktoken (OpenAI's free, open-source tokenizer that runs entirely on your machine, no API key needed):

tokenizer_peek.py 
import tiktoken

encoding = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")  # GPT-4-era tokenizer

text = "supercalifragilisticexpialidocious"
tokens = encoding.encode(text)
pieces = [encoding.decode([t]) for t in tokens]

print(f"Token count: {len(tokens)}")
print(f"Split into:  {pieces}")

Thought process: Before writing code to "see the tokens," an experienced dev realizes a token id (an integer) isn't human-readable on its own — the useful move is to decode each token back to its own text piece so you can literally see where the word got cut. The one gotcha to plan for: get_encoding may need to fetch a vocabulary file the first time, so the code should fail gracefully if there's no network rather than crash with a raw stack trace.

What is... the "letter counting" weirdness? People are baffled when an LLM struggles with "how many letters are in 'strawberry'?" The model isn't bad at counting — it never actually sees the individual letters. It sees tokens like straw and berry, and it's trying to reason about letters using units that don't map cleanly onto letters at all. It's like asking someone to count bricks in a wall when they can only perceive pre-assembled chunks of 3-4 bricks at a time. Once you know this, the failure stops being mysterious.
Go Deeper: why the vocabulary was "learned," not designed

Nobody sat down and hand-wrote a list of 100,000 tokens. The vocabulary is learned from training text by an algorithm (byte-pair encoding) that repeatedly merges the most frequently co-occurring character sequences into single tokens. That's why the splits are statistically driven rather than linguistically clean: a rare word gets chopped into whatever common sub-pieces the algorithm happened to build, not into tidy dictionary syllables.

Quick recap
  • An LLM processes tokens, not words or letters.
  • Common words are often single tokens; rare words split into sub-word pieces.
  • Non-English text often uses more tokens for the same meaning — and costs more.
  • Letter-counting failures happen because the model never sees individual letters.

2. Embeddings — turning words into math, intuitively

Computers fundamentally work with numbers, not words. So before any of the "next token prediction" magic from Session 1.1 can happen, every token needs to be converted into a list of numbers. That list is called an embedding.

What is... an embedding? An embedding is a token turned into a long list of numbers — but not arbitrary numbers. They're constructed during training so that words with similar meaning end up with similar numbers, close to each other in a very high-dimensional space.

Picture a map — but instead of plotting cities by physical location, you're plotting words by meaning. On this map:

  • "king" and "queen" land near each other (both royalty-related).
  • "dog" and "puppy" land very close together (closely related meaning).
  • "dog" and "astronomy" land far apart (unrelated concepts).
  • "happy" and "joyful" practically overlap.
  • "happy" and "miserable" land in a meaningfully different direction — they share emotional context while differing in valence.

A real embedding isn't a 2D map — it's typically hundreds or thousands of dimensions — but the idea is identical: words and concepts that mean similar things end up mathematically close together. This is what lets a model "know" that "physician" and "doctor" are related without anyone programming that rule. It learned it from seeing those words used in similar contexts millions of times.

What is... the king − man + woman example? Take the embedding for "king," mathematically subtract "man," and add "woman" — you land very close to "queen." The model never learned that as a rule; the relationship emerged from how those words get used across enormous amounts of text. It's the clearest sign these numbers capture something real about meaning, not just arbitrary IDs.
Go Deeper: where you'll actually use this hands-on

This isn't just theory. Embeddings are the literal foundation of RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation), the entire subject of Week 3. When you build a system that finds "the most relevant document chunk for this question," what's happening under the hood is: convert the question to an embedding, convert all the document chunks to embeddings, and find which chunks are mathematically closest to the question's embedding. Everything in Week 3 builds directly on the intuition you're forming right now.

Quick recap
  • An embedding turns a token into a list of numbers that captures meaning.
  • Similar-meaning words end up mathematically close together.
  • This is learned from training data, not explicitly programmed.
  • Embeddings are the foundation of RAG (Week 3).
Sub-topic 3 of 4

3. Context windows — the model's working memory

Imagine you're working at a desk, and you can only have so many papers spread out in front of you at once. You can't use anything that isn't physically on the desk right now — even if it's in a filing cabinet in the next room, you'd have to go get it and bring it back before you could use it.

What is... a context window? A context window is the model's desk: the maximum amount of text — measured in tokens, not words — that the model can "see" and consider at once when generating a response. Everything outside that window simply isn't visible to the model in that moment.

Context windows have grown enormously — from a few thousand tokens in early models to hundreds of thousands (an entire book's worth) in modern systems. But a bigger desk doesn't mean every paper on it gets equal attention. Practically, this shows up in three ways:

  • Long conversations eventually "forget" early details — not human-style bad memory, but because once a conversation exceeds the context window, the earliest parts genuinely fall off the desk and are no longer visible.
  • Stuffing irrelevant information in can hurt quality even when there's technically room. This is often called "context rot" or the "lost in the middle" problem: information buried in the middle of a long context gets less effective attention than information near the beginning or end. You'll see this concretely in Week 3, Session 3.5 (RAG Failure Modes).
  • This is why prompt engineering (all of Week 2) matters so much — what you put on the desk, and how you arrange it, directly shapes output quality. A cluttered, poorly organized desk produces worse work even from a brilliant person.
Quick recap
  • A context window is the model's working memory, measured in tokens.
  • Anything outside the window is invisible to the model.
  • Information in the middle of a long context can get less attention ("lost in the middle").
  • More context isn't automatically better — arrangement matters.
Sub-topic 4 of 4

4. Putting the three pieces together

Let's walk end to end through what actually happens when you type a message to an LLM. Take "What's the capital of France?":

The five-step loop 
1. Tokenize    "What's the capital of France?"
               -> What | 's | the | capital | of | France | ?

2. Embed       each token -> a long list of numbers capturing its meaning

3. Fit in      all embeddings sit in the context window, alongside the
   context      conversation history and any system instructions

4. Predict     the model predicts the next token, using everything currently
               in the context window (exactly as in Session 1.1)

5. Repeat      the predicted token is added to the sequence, and steps 4-5
               repeat token by token until a "stop" signal or length limit

Every single LLM response you've ever seen, no matter how impressive, is this five-step loop running over and over. There's no separate "thinking" step hidden somewhere else — the entire process is right here, and now you understand every piece of it.

Quick recap
  • The full loop is: tokenize → embed → consider everything in the context window → predict next token → repeat.
  • Generation is token by token, until a stop signal or length limit.
  • There's no hidden extra "thinking" step beyond this loop.
In the Real World
  • Every time you compare LLM providers on price, you're comparing per-token rates — so tokenization efficiency (this session's Part 1) is literally a line item in your cloud bill, and non-English products can cost more to run for the exact same feature.
  • Production RAG systems — the standard way companies get an LLM to answer questions over their own documents — are built entirely on embeddings: retrieve the chunks whose embeddings are closest to the question, then feed those into the context window.
  • "The bot forgot what I told it earlier" support tickets are almost always a context window limit, not a bug — and "we gave it more context and it got worse" is the very real "lost in the middle" effect, not bad luck.

Points to Remember

  • Tokens are the actual unit an LLM processes — not words, not letters. Common words are often single tokens; rare words get split into sub-word pieces. This is why some languages use more tokens (and cost more) for equivalent meaning.
  • Embeddings convert tokens into numbers that capture meaning — words with similar meanings end up mathematically close together. Learned from patterns in training data, not explicitly programmed.
  • A context window is the model's "desk" — the maximum text it can consider at once. Information outside it is invisible; information buried in the middle can get less effective attention than information near the edges.
  • The full loop is: tokenize → embed → consider the context window → predict next token → repeat. There's no hidden extra "thinking" step.
  • This explains real, observable behaviors: letter-counting struggles, conversations "forgetting" early details, and why crowding a prompt with irrelevant context can hurt rather than help.
