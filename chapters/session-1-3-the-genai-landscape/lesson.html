Session 1.3 Beginner 25–30 min read

The GenAI Landscape

You know what generative AI is and roughly how it works. Now the practical question: when you actually have to build something, which model do you reach for — and why?

On this page 1. The major players 2. Dimensions that matter 3. A decision framework 4. A worked comparison
The GenAI landscape changes constantly — new models launch what feels like every few weeks, benchmarks shuffle, prices shift. A "best model" ranking would be wrong within months. So this session teaches something more durable: the categories, the trade-off dimensions, and the decision framework that stay useful long after today's specific model names are replaced by their successors. A working professional doesn't memorize a leaderboard — they know how to evaluate whatever's on it.
Sub-topic 1 of 4

1. The major players — a map, not a leaderboard

Ranking models goes stale immediately. Understanding the kinds of organizations building them does not — it tells you something durable about their incentives and strengths. The landscape splits into two camps.

What is... a closed/proprietary API provider? A company that trains large, frontier-capability models and makes them available only through an API or chatbot product — you can never download the weights or run the model yourself. The names you hear constantly: OpenAI (GPT, ChatGPT), Anthropic (Claude), and Google (Gemini).

What unites the closed providers: they pour enormous resources into training the largest, most capable models, and monetize through API usage fees and subscriptions. The trade you make with them:

  • You get convenience — no infrastructure to manage, frontier capability, automatic improvements.
  • You give up control over how the model runs, where your data goes during processing, and the ability to customize the model's internals.
What is... an open-weight provider? An organization that releases the actual trained model — its parameters, the literal "weights" — for anyone to download and run on their own hardware. Meta (the Llama family) is the most prominent, alongside Mistral and DeepSeek.

"Open" here is a specific, technical claim. It doesn't always mean "open source" in the traditional software sense — training data and training code are often still private — but it does mean you can:

  • self-host the model on your own infrastructure,
  • fine-tune it on your own data,
  • and guarantee your data never leaves your infrastructure during inference.

The cost: you (or your company) now own all the infrastructure complexity — GPUs, serving software, scaling, monitoring — that the closed providers handle invisibly for you.

Go Deeper: why this split isn't going away

This isn't a temporary market quirk — it reflects a genuine tension. Building frontier models costs an enormous amount in compute and research talent, which pushes providers toward charging for access (closed). But many organizations — regulated industries like healthcare and finance, or anyone with strict data-residency rules — have a hard requirement that data never transits a third party's servers, which only open, self-hostable models satisfy. Expect both categories to keep existing, growing, and competing indefinitely: they solve for genuinely different constraints, not the same axis.

Quick recap
  • Closed/proprietary (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google): frontier capability, zero infrastructure burden, but no control and no data-location guarantee.
  • Open-weight (Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek): self-hostable, customizable, data stays put — but you own all the operational complexity.
  • The split is permanent because the two camps solve different constraints.
Sub-topic 2 of 4

2. The dimensions that actually matter for choosing

Forget "which model is best" — that question is incomplete. The real question is always: best for what, given which constraints? Six dimensions should drive any real decision.

What is... capability? How well the model handles complex reasoning, nuanced writing, coding, or multi-step problems. This is what most public benchmarks measure — it's genuinely important, but it's the dimension most likely to change month to month and the one most overweighted by newcomers. A model that's 2% better on a benchmark is rarely worth a 5x cost increase for most real applications.

Speed (latency) — how quickly the model responds (tokens per second) and how long before the first token appears. This matters enormously for real-time, user-facing apps (a chatbot someone is actively waiting on) and far less for batch or asynchronous work (overnight reports, a document queue). Smaller, lighter models are almost always faster — which is why most providers offer a tiered lineup: a small/fast model, a mid-tier model, and a large/frontier model.

Cost is priced per million tokens, split into input and output cost — output is usually priced higher, since generating text is more computationally expensive than reading it. Costs vary by an order of magnitude or more between a provider's cheapest and most capable tiers. Self-hosting an open-weight model trades per-token fees for fixed infrastructure costs, which only becomes economical at high, predictable volume.

What is... context length? How much text the model can consider in a single request. It has grown dramatically — models now commonly handle context windows in the hundreds of thousands to low millions of tokens. A huge window matters enormously for analyzing an entire codebase or a large collection of legal documents in one pass, and far less for short conversational tasks.

Specialization — some models or variants are tuned for specific tasks: strong coding, strong multilingual handling, or dedicated "reasoning" modes that spend more computation deliberating before answering. A generalist powerhouse isn't automatically best for a narrow task, where a smaller, tuned model might do just as well for a fraction of the cost.

Compliance and data handling — for regulated industries (healthcare, finance, government) this can be the deciding factor above all others, overriding capability comparisons entirely. Questions like "does this provider offer a signed compliance agreement for sensitive health data?" or "where geographically is my data processed and stored?" can eliminate otherwise-excellent options immediately. Always verify compliance commitments directly with a provider's enterprise sales channel — documentation pages lag reality.

Quick recap
  • Capability — most talked about, most overweighted.
  • Speed — critical for real-time apps, near-irrelevant for batch jobs.
  • Cost — per-million-token input/output; varies 10x+ across tiers.
  • Context length — matters for whole-codebase / whole-document tasks.
  • Specialization — a tuned smaller model can beat a generalist for a narrow task.
  • Compliance — can override everything else for regulated data.

3. A practical decision framework

Here's a process you can actually apply, regardless of which specific models exist when you read this.

The four steps 
Step 1  Start with a strong, well-known general-purpose model
        (build the working version first; don't optimize prematurely)
           |
Step 2  Ask: do you need that much capability for production?
        (test a smaller/faster/cheaper tier explicitly — don't assume)
           |
Step 3  Check for HARD constraints that override capability
        (regulated data? self-hosting for residency? fixed high volume?)
           |
Step 4  Re-evaluate periodically, not constantly
        (quarterly is common — re-architecting every launch is waste)

Step 1 — start strong and general. Don't optimize prematurely. Build your first working version with whichever capable, well-documented model is easiest to get started with. Get the actual application logic right first.

Step 2 — question the capability you're paying for. Many tasks that feel like they need the most powerful model actually work fine on a smaller, faster, cheaper tier from the same provider. Test this explicitly — don't assume.

Step 3 — check hard constraints. Does this involve regulated data? Does the use case require self-hosting for data residency? Is there a fixed, predictable high volume that would make self-hosting an open-weight model cheaper than ongoing API fees? These can eliminate "the best model" entirely in favor of "the only compliant model."

Step 4 — re-evaluate on a cadence. The landscape moves fast, but re-architecting every time a new model launches is its own kind of waste. Pick a reasonable rhythm (quarterly is common) to reassess whether a newer or cheaper option now fits better.

Quick recap
  • Build first with a strong general model; get the logic right before optimizing.
  • Then test whether a cheaper/faster tier is good enough — measure, don't assume.
  • Let hard constraints (compliance, residency, volume) override pure capability.
  • Reassess on a cadence, not on every launch.
Sub-topic 4 of 4

4. A worked comparison

Let's make the trade-offs concrete. Imagine three applications. Notice how the "right" model choice differs sharply for each, even though all three are technically just "calling an LLM."

  • App A — a customer-facing live chat widget on an e-commerce site. Priority: speed (users won't wait), moderate capability (most questions are simple), cost matters at high volume. → A fast, cheap, smaller-tier closed model. Frontier capability is wasted; latency is what users feel.
  • App B — an overnight batch job summarizing lengthy legal contracts. Priority: capability and context length (contracts are long; a missed clause is costly), speed barely matters (runs unattended), cost matters less per call. → A frontier-capability, large-context model is worth it, even slower and pricier per call.
  • App C — a healthcare clinical documentation assistant handling protected patient data. Priority: compliance first, full stop — capability and cost only get evaluated among the options that already satisfy compliance. → Filter to providers with confirmed compliance agreements, then compare within that set.

The pattern: none of these three would make the same model choice, and none is simply "pick whichever model wins this month's benchmark." That's the actual skill this session teaches.

In the exercise, you'll capture exactly this kind of comparison in code — one dictionary per model, holding the dimensions you observed — then let a small script format the table and prompt you for a recommendation. Here's the shape of that data:

model_comparison.py 
# One dict per model you tested, on the SAME task
RESULTS = [
    {
        "model_name": "Smaller/faster tier",
        "provider": "Provider A",
        "response_time_seconds": 1.2,   # stopwatch is fine
        "quality_rating": 4,            # your own 1-5 judgment
        "cost_per_million_input": 0.25,
        "cost_per_million_output": 1.25,
    },
    {
        "model_name": "Larger/frontier tier",
        "provider": "Provider A",
        "response_time_seconds": 3.8,   # ~3x slower
        "quality_rating": 5,            # slightly richer answer
        "cost_per_million_input": 3.00,
        "cost_per_million_output": 15.00,   # ~10x pricier output
    },
]

Quick recap
  • The same three-word task ("call an LLM") can demand three totally different models.
  • Chat widget → speed. Batch summarizer → capability + context. Health data → compliance first.
  • Capturing observations as uniform per-model dicts makes the trade-off comparable and repeatable.
In the Real World
  • Teams routinely run different models across different features of the same product — a cheap fast tier for autocomplete and confirmation messages, a frontier tier for the hard reasoning feature — because there's no "house model" requirement, only per-task fit.
  • Regulated industries (healthcare, finance, government) often self-host open-weight models specifically so patient or financial data never transits a third party — compliance, not capability, drives that architecture.
  • Production teams reassess model choice on a quarterly cadence, not per launch — the same "measure, don't assume; re-evaluate, don't churn" discipline this session teaches, applied as a standing process.

Points to Remember

  • The landscape splits into closed/proprietary (frontier capability, no infrastructure burden, but no control or data-location guarantee) and open-weight (self-hostable, customizable, data stays in your infrastructure, but you own the operational complexity).
  • "Best model" is an incomplete question. Ask "best for what task, under which constraints (speed, cost, context length, compliance)?"
  • Capability is the most talked-about and most overweighted dimension. Speed, cost, and compliance often matter more than a small benchmark edge.
  • Compliance and data-handling requirements can override every other consideration for regulated industries — verify directly with a provider's enterprise team.
  • Different applications in the same company can reasonably use entirely different models — matching the model to the task's constraints is the actual skill.
