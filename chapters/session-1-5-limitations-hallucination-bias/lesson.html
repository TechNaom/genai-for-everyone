Session 1.5 · Week 1 Foundations 25–30 min read

Limitations, Hallucination & Bias

The same mechanism that makes these models so capable also makes them capable of being confidently, fluently, convincingly wrong. This session is about respecting what GenAI actually is — so you build for its real failure modes instead of getting blindsided by them after launch.

On this page 1. Hallucination — confident, fluent, and wrong 2. Where bias enters the pipeline 3. Other real limitations to know cold 4. A realistic scenario, worked through
You've now built a real, working GenAI application. It runs. It responds. It feels like magic. This session exists to make sure that feeling doesn't turn into misplaced trust. Every serious GenAI practitioner — and every serious interview for a GenAI role — assumes you understand this material cold. This isn't a session about being afraid of GenAI; it's about knowing exactly where and why it fails, so you can design around it.
Sub-topic 1 of 4

1. Hallucination — confident, fluent, and wrong

Hallucination, in the GenAI sense, means the model generates content that is factually incorrect, fabricated, or unsupported — while presenting it with the same fluent confidence as accurate content. The output isn't hedged, isn't uncertain-sounding, isn't flagged in any way as potentially wrong. It just is wrong, delivered with total conversational ease.

What is... hallucination? The one distinction that matters: fluency and accuracy are two separate properties of the output. The word is a little dramatic (the model isn't "seeing things"), but it captures something real — nothing about how the model works guarantees that a confident-sounding answer is a correct one.

We've actually already built the explanation for this over the last few sessions. Assembling it makes hallucination stop being mysterious:

  • From Session 1.1: an LLM generates the most statistically plausible next token, given everything before it — it does not retrieve verified facts from a database unless explicitly given a tool to do so.
  • From Session 1.2: the model's "knowledge" lives in the patterns learned across its training data, compressed into relationships between tokens and embeddings — not as a lookup table of discrete, verifiable facts with sources attached.

Put these together: when you ask a model something where the most statistically plausible continuation happens to be false, the model has no internal mechanism that flags this and says "wait, I'm not sure." It produces the plausible-sounding pattern, because that is all it was ever built to do.

What is... the classic fabricated-citation pattern? Ask a model for the page number of a quote from an obscure book, or a citation for a very specific statistic, and it will often produce something that looks exactly like a real citation — a plausible author, a plausible journal, a specific page — entirely fabricated. "A citation-shaped piece of text" is precisely the most statistically plausible continuation, even when no such source exists. This is one of the most consequential, well-documented hallucination patterns in real deployments, especially in legal and academic work.
Go Deeper: why the next model version won't simply "fix" this

Later, larger models do generally hallucinate less often on many tasks — but hallucination is not a temporary engineering bug awaiting a patch. It's a structural consequence of how these models generate text at all. Newer models reduce the frequency, not the fundamental possibility. Any system design that assumes "the next model release will solve this" is building on a false premise. The real mitigations — grounding outputs in retrieved, verifiable documents (Week 3), rigorous evaluation (Week 5), and guardrails (also Week 5) — are about managing this reality, not eliminating it.

Quick recap
  • Hallucination is fabricated content delivered with the same fluent confidence as accurate content.
  • It happens because the model predicts plausible next tokens, with no internal "am I sure?" check.
  • Fluency and accuracy are separate properties — confidence tells you nothing about correctness.
  • It's a structural property, not a bug the next model release will patch away.

2. Where bias enters the pipeline

A common misconception is that "AI bias" means one thing: biased training data, full stop. The reality is more important to understand — bias can enter a GenAI system at multiple distinct points, and fixing only one while ignoring the others leaves real problems unsolved.

What is... a "bias entry point"? A stage in the GenAI pipeline where skew can be introduced into the final output. The key insight is that there are at least four of them, not one — so "is our AI biased?" is a question you ask at every stage, not just about where the model came from.
  • Training data bias. If the text a model learned from over- or under-represents certain perspectives, demographics, languages, or viewpoints, its outputs tend to reflect those same imbalances. This is the source most people have heard of — but it's far from the only one.
  • Prompt-level bias. The way a question is phrased can shift the output in a biased direction, even with identical underlying facts. "Why do many people distrust [group]" presupposes something different than "what are perspectives on [group]," and the answer reflects the framing baked into the question.
  • Few-shot example bias. When you give a model example input/output pairs to guide its behavior (a Week 2 technique), the specific examples you choose can introduce skew. If every "successful entrepreneur" example shares certain characteristics, the model may implicitly learn to associate success with those characteristics, regardless of your intent.
  • Evaluation bias. Even checking whether output is "good" can encode bias — if the criteria or the human reviewers share a narrow set of assumptions, outputs that diverge may get unfairly penalized. Evaluation isn't a neutral, bias-free step just because it happens after generation (much more in Week 5).

If your organization's response to "is our AI biased?" is solely "we checked the training data," you've addressed exactly one of at least four entry points. A careful practitioner asks at every stage: how was this prompted, what examples shaped its behavior, and how is "good output" being judged.

Quick recap
  • Bias is not a single point of failure — it enters at multiple distinct stages.
  • The four entry points: training data, prompt phrasing, few-shot example selection, and evaluation.
  • "We checked the training data" addresses only one of at least four.
Sub-topic 3 of 4

3. Other real limitations to know cold

Beyond hallucination and bias, a few other limitations come up constantly in real GenAI work and in interviews. Knowing these separates someone who has genuinely used these systems from someone who has only read about them.

What is... a knowledge cutoff? Every model has a point in time after which it has no training data at all — it has never seen anything that happened after that date. Ask about something genuinely recent, and the model either says so honestly, or — more concerning — hallucinates a plausible-sounding but fabricated answer rather than admitting uncertainty. This is exactly the problem Week 3's Retrieval-Augmented Generation exists to solve.
  • Inconsistency across runs. Ask the exact same question twice and you may get meaningfully different answers, especially for open-ended tasks. This isn't necessarily a flaw — some randomness is often intentional and adjustable (a setting called "temperature") — but it does mean you can't always assume perfect reproducibility, which matters for testing and evaluation.
  • Struggles with precise counting and certain logic. Letter-counting and similar tasks are awkward for token-based models because the units they process don't map cleanly onto the units the task needs. Models can also struggle with deeply nested logic or long chains of precise arithmetic — even while excelling at tasks that "feel" much harder, like nuanced prose. Capability isn't evenly distributed the way human intuition expects.
  • Sensitivity to phrasing. The same underlying question, asked two slightly different ways, can produce meaningfully different quality of response. This isn't something to be embarrassed about as a user — it's a real property of how these systems work, and it's the entire reason prompt engineering (all of Week 2) exists as a discipline.
Quick recap
  • A knowledge cutoff means no training data after a fixed date — RAG (Week 3) is the structural fix.
  • Run-to-run inconsistency is often intentional randomness ("temperature"), not always a bug.
  • Token-based models are awkward at precise counting and long chains of exact logic.
  • Sensitivity to phrasing is why prompt engineering exists as a discipline.

4. A realistic scenario, worked through

The setup: your company just launched an AI feature that answers customer questions about your product. Three days in, a customer posts on social media that the bot confidently told them your product supports a feature it does not actually have. Your product manager is panicking and asks you to explain what happened and what you're going to do.

What is... the calm, accurate explanation? The model wasn't being deceptive. It generated the most statistically plausible-sounding answer about your product's features — likely drawing on general knowledge of what similar products typically offer — rather than a verified fact about your specific product. Without being connected to your actual, current documentation, it has no way to distinguish "what's generally true of products like this" from "what's specifically true of this exact product." This is hallucination, exactly as in Sub-topic 1 — expected behavior of an ungrounded model, not a one-off glitch.

When your PM asks you to write the internal explanation, this is a genuine "direct an AI assistant" moment — the kind of drafting task you'll do constantly. Here's how an experienced practitioner would approach it before typing a single word to the model.

Thought process: The explanation has to do three things at once — stay non-technical, be honest that no current LLM eliminates this risk, and point to concrete mitigations rather than vague reassurance. The trap to avoid is promising it will "never happen again," because that promise can't be honestly made about any current GenAI system. So the prompt must explicitly forbid that promise and name the real fix (grounding in documentation) instead of hand-waving at "a smarter model."

The honest framing for your PM: no current LLM eliminates this risk entirely. What reduces it dramatically is grounding the model's answers in your actual product documentation — having it retrieve and cite real, current information rather than generating from general patterns. That's RAG, the entire subject of Week 3, and it's the direct, structural fix for exactly this failure — not a vague promise that "the next model update will be smarter."

Quick recap
  • Explain the mechanism calmly: an ungrounded model produced a plausible pattern, not a verified fact.
  • Be honest — no current LLM eliminates this risk entirely.
  • Never promise it will "never happen again"; commit to specific mitigations instead.
  • The structural fix is grounding (RAG), plus evaluation and guardrails.
In the Real World
  • Lawyers have been sanctioned for filing briefs containing AI-fabricated case citations that looked completely real — the exact citation-shaped-text hallucination pattern, with career consequences.
  • "Is our AI biased? We checked the training data" is a real, incomplete answer given in real product reviews — the multi-entry-point view is what turns a checkbox into genuine diligence.
  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation (Week 3), evaluation (Week 5), and guardrails (Week 5) are not academic add-ons — they are the direct, structural mitigations teams actually ship to manage the limitations in this session.

Points to Remember

  • Hallucination is confident, fluent, fabricated content — a structural consequence of predicting plausible continuations, not a temporary bug awaiting a patch.
  • Bias can enter at multiple points: training data, prompt phrasing, few-shot example selection, and evaluation itself — fixing one leaves the others unexamined.
  • Knowledge cutoffs, run-to-run inconsistency, awkward counting/logic, and phrasing sensitivity are all real, well-documented limitations, separate from hallucination and bias.
  • The professional response to an AI failure is calm, mechanism-based explanation plus concrete mitigation — never a promise that it will "never happen again."
  • Grounding (RAG, Week 3), evaluation (Week 5), and guardrails (Week 5) are the real, structural mitigations — not "wait for a smarter model."
