Interview Questions: Synthesizing Week 1

These are scenario-based questions that pull together everything from Week 1 — the kind an interviewer uses to check whether you can actually scope an AI problem rather than just define terms. Interviewers consistently say they're listening for trade-off reasoning and concrete examples, not textbook recall. Each question includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 Your manager says: "Let's add AI to our checkout flow to stop fraud." Walk me through how you'd scope this.
Strong answer

First, recognize that this is most likely a predictive AI problem — classification or scoring of transactions — not a generative one, so an LLM may not be the right tool at all. Then show the discipline of asking clarifying questions before writing any code: what does "stop fraud" mean operationally (block, flag, or review?), what labelled data is available, and what's the relative cost of a false positive (blocking a legitimate customer) versus a false negative (letting fraud through)? The scoping conversation matters more than the model choice.

Red flags

Jumping straight to "we'll use an LLM" without questioning whether the problem is even generative, or starting to design a solution without asking a single clarifying question about data, definitions, or error costs.

Follow-up

"Which is worse for this business — a false positive or a false negative — and how would that change where you set the decision threshold?"

2 Give an example of a business request that sounds like it needs generative AI but is actually better solved with a simpler, non-AI approach.
Strong answer

A concrete example works best: e.g., an "auto-reply to common support emails" request where the replies are actually a handful of fixed responses keyed to a category — a templated, rules-based auto-responder does the job reliably, cheaply, and predictably, with no LLM at all. The reasoning is that added complexity (API cost, latency, hallucination risk, evaluation burden) isn't justified when a simpler tool does the job just as well. Reaching for an LLM by default is a common and expensive mistake.

Red flags

Being unable to produce a concrete example, treating "AI" as automatically better, or ignoring the real costs an LLM adds (latency, unpredictability, the need to guard against hallucination).

Follow-up

"What would have to change about the requirements before an LLM genuinely became the right tool for that same task?"

Strategy Tips

  • For scenario questions, think out loud: naming your assumptions and asking clarifying questions is the answer interviewers want — scoping discipline is the skill being tested, not a memorized definition.
  • When asked "would you use AI here?", the strong move is often to argue for the simplest tool that works. Showing you won't over-engineer with an LLM is itself a senior signal.
