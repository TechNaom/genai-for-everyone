Session 2.1 Beginner 25–30 min read

Anatomy of a Great Prompt

Week 1 gave you the mental model. Week 2 starts turning that model into a repeatable skill — and this is the single most immediately useful, immediately testable chapter in the whole program.

On this page 1. Why "Just Ask Nicely" Isn't a Strategy 2. The Four Pillars of a Great Prompt 3. A Worked Rewrite, Pillar by Pillar 4. When More Detail Stops Helping
In Week 1's final session you wrote system prompts by feel — intuition about what a "beginner" explanation versus an "expert" explanation should contain. That intuition got you through, but it doesn't scale, doesn't transfer cleanly to a new task, and is nearly impossible to debug when a prompt goes wrong and you can't say why. This chapter replaces "feel" with a checklist you can run on any prompt, for any task, every single time. It also happens to be exactly what real interviews probe hardest — you will very likely be handed a bad prompt live and asked to fix it on the spot. By the end of this chapter, that's a routine you can run without flinching.
Sub-topic 1 of 4

1. Why "Just Ask Nicely" Isn't a Strategy

Here's a common, costly habit: treating an LLM prompt like a Google search query — a few keywords, minimal structure, trusting that "the AI is smart enough to figure out what I mean." This works sometimes, for simple requests with one obvious interpretation. It fails constantly for anything with real ambiguity — and the failure is subtle. You don't get an error message. You get a confident, fluent, wrong-for-your-purposes answer, because the model genuinely had no way to know what you actually wanted from the information you gave it.

prompt-a-vs-prompt-b.txt 
Prompt A: "Write something about our product."

Prompt B: "Write a 100-word product description for our wireless
noise-canceling headphones, targeting busy professionals who travel
frequently. Emphasize battery life and comfort during long flights.
Tone: confident but not salesy. Format: a single paragraph, no
bullet points."

Both are grammatically valid English. Only one gives the model enough to work with to produce something actually useful on the first try. Prompt A isn't wrong — the model will produce something — but that something is essentially a guess. Every missing detail (which product? which audience? what tone? what length?) gets filled in with whatever's statistically most generic, because nothing in the prompt steered it elsewhere.

What is... a prompt, really? A prompt is not a question — it's a specification. You are not asking the model what it thinks; you're specifying what you need produced, with enough detail that a competent person reading only that specification could produce something close to what you want. If you wouldn't hand Prompt A to a freelance writer and expect a usable result, you shouldn't expect one from an LLM either — and for the same reason: critical information is simply missing, not implied.
Quick recap
  • Treating a prompt like a search query works only for simple, unambiguous requests.
  • Missing detail isn't intelligently inferred — it's filled in with whatever's statistically generic.
  • A prompt is a specification, not a question: could a competent stranger produce what you need from it alone?

2. The Four Pillars of a Great Prompt

Every effective prompt, regardless of task, tends to cover four distinct dimensions. Missing any one of them is usually where a "not quite right" output comes from — and naming which pillar is missing is exactly the diagnostic skill this session builds.

Pillar 1: Clarity Clarity means the core task is unambiguous. Not "write about X" but "write a [specific thing] that does [specific job]." Vague verbs ("discuss," "talk about," "cover") are a warning sign — they describe an activity, not a deliverable. Specific verbs ("summarize," "compare," "draft," "classify," "translate") describe an actual output.
pillar-1-clarity.txt 
Weak:  "Talk about the meeting."
Clear: "Summarize the key decisions and action items from this
        meeting transcript in 5 bullet points."
Pillar 2: Context Context means giving the model the background information it needs to make good decisions — information that isn't part of the instruction itself, but shapes what a good answer looks like. This connects directly to Session 1.2's context window concept: context is literally what you're choosing to put on the model's "desk."
pillar-2-context.txt 
Without context: "Write a follow-up email."
  (Follow-up to what? About what? To whom?)

With context: "Write a follow-up email to a client who attended our
  product demo last week but hasn't responded since. We offered a
  10% discount during the call that's still on the table."
Pillar 3: Constraints Constraints define the boundaries of the output — length, format, tone, what to include, and importantly, what to exclude. Constraints are where most "technically correct but unusable" outputs get fixed. A model given no length constraint might write 50 words or 500; a model given no format constraint might return prose when you needed a table.
pillar-3-constraints.txt 
"Keep it under 150 words."
"Respond only in valid JSON."
"Don't mention pricing."
"Write in second person."
"Use no more than 2 sentences per paragraph."
Pillar 4: Format Specification Format specification is related to constraints but deserves its own focus, because it's so often the difference between an answer you can use and one you have to rework. If you need structured output (a list, a table, JSON, a specific template), say so explicitly and show the shape if it's not obvious. Don't assume the model will guess the exact structure your downstream code or workflow needs.
pillar-4-format.txt 
Vague:     "Give me the pros and cons."
Specified: "Give me the pros and cons as two separate bulleted
            lists, with a maximum of 4 points each, under the
            headers 'Pros' and 'Cons'."
The one-line version
  • Clarity: what's the deliverable? Context: what does the model need to know? Constraints: what are the boundaries? Format: what shape does the output need to be?
  • Most disappointing outputs trace back to exactly one of these four being missing — not a "dumb" model.
Quick recap
  • Clarity = an unambiguous core task, driven by a specific verb.
  • Context = the background the model needs to make good decisions.
  • Constraints = the boundaries on length, tone, and content.
  • Format = the actual shape the output needs to take.

3. A Worked Rewrite, Pillar by Pillar

Let's take a genuinely bad prompt and rebuild it methodically, naming which pillar each addition addresses. This is the exact move you'll be asked to perform live in an interview.

rewrite_walkthrough.txt 
Starting prompt: "Help with customer email."
  -> fails on every pillar at once: no task, no background,
     no boundaries, no shape.

+ Clarity
  "Draft a reply to a customer email."
  -> now we know the deliverable is a REPLY, not advice or analysis.

+ Context
  "Draft a reply to a customer who emailed asking for a refund on a
  product that arrived damaged. They've been a customer for 3 years
  and this is their first complaint."
  -> the model now knows the situation and the customer's history,
     and can calibrate tone: a loyal first-time complainer deserves
     a different response than a chronic one.

+ Constraints
  "...Approve the refund, apologize for the inconvenience, and offer
  a 15% discount on their next order as a goodwill gesture. Keep the
  tone warm and personal, not corporate. Maximum 120 words."
  -> now the model knows the exact OUTCOME to deliver, not just the
     situation.

+ Format
  "...Format as a complete email including a subject line."
  -> the output is immediately usable, not a paragraph you have to
     manually turn into an email.

Final prompt: "Draft a reply to a customer who emailed asking for a refund on a product that arrived damaged. They've been a customer for 3 years and this is their first complaint. Approve the refund, apologize for the inconvenience, and offer a 15% discount on their next order as a goodwill gesture. Keep the tone warm and personal, not corporate. Maximum 120 words. Format as a complete email including a subject line."

Notice this isn't a longer prompt for the sake of length — every addition did real work. That's the difference between padding and precision, and it's the test you should apply to your own prompts before you consider them "done": does this sentence remove a guess, or just add words?

Thought process: before asking an AI assistant to help you build a reusable prompt-rewrite habit into code, an experienced practitioner decides the schema first: for each weak prompt, capture which pillar(s) are missing, the rewritten version, and a one-sentence justification. That schema forces the same diagnostic reasoning you just walked through above, on every row, instead of letting "make it better" stay vague.

Write the prompt you'd give an AI assistant to build this:

Paste your prompt into Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, or whatever assistant you use, and compare what it builds against this lesson's example — does it force an explicit missing-pillar diagnosis for every row, or does it just produce prettier prose? That comparison is the real test, not a score.
Show a strong version of this prompt 
Write a small Python script that defines a list of dictionaries called WEAK_PROMPTS. Each dictionary has four keys: "weak_prompt" (a genuinely vague real-world prompt), "missing_pillars" (a list containing any of "clarity", "context", "constraints", "format"), "rewritten_prompt" (an improved version that closes those specific gaps), and "why_better" (one sentence explaining what changed and why it matters). Include a function that prints each entry and warns if any missing_pillars value is not one of the four valid pillar names. Do not use any external libraries or API calls -- it's a pure prompt-design reasoning script.
Quick recap
  • Rebuild a bad prompt one pillar at a time, and be able to say out loud which pillar each addition fixes.
  • Every addition should remove a guess the model would otherwise have to make — not just add words.
  • This exact "narrate your rewrite" move is the most common live-exercise format in real prompt-engineering interviews.

4. When More Detail Stops Helping

A reasonable question at this point: doesn't there come a point where you're just over-engineering a prompt for a simple task? Yes — and recognizing that point is itself part of the skill.

Signal that you've added enough Could a competent person, reading only your prompt, produce something close to what you actually need, without having to guess at anything important? If yes, you're done — further additions are diminishing returns at best, and at worst can clutter the prompt in ways that actually hurt focus. This is Session 1.2's "lost in the middle" effect showing up again, now inside a single prompt instead of a long document.
Signal that you need more The model's output technically answers the prompt but isn't usable, and when you look back at your prompt, you can identify a specific piece of information you knew but never actually told the model. That gap — something you knew that the model had no way to know — is almost always the fix.
Why length lies
  • A prompt can be long, well-formatted, and detailed-looking while still missing the one piece of context that actually mattered — length is not the same thing as completeness.
  • This is the trap the Pro path project in this session is built around: a prompt that reads as thorough on first glance, but fails a real business task because of a single missing detail.
  • The practical habit: don't judge a prompt by its word count. Judge it by whether a reader could act on it without guessing.
Quick recap
  • "Enough detail" = a competent reader could act on the prompt without guessing at anything important.
  • If output is unusable, look for a specific fact you knew but never told the model — that's usually the whole fix.
  • A long, polished-looking prompt can still be missing one critical piece of context; length doesn't guarantee completeness.

Points to Remember

  • A prompt is a specification, not a question. Missing information doesn't get intelligently inferred — it gets filled in with whatever's statistically generic, which is usually not what you wanted.
  • Four pillars: clarity (unambiguous core task), context (background the model needs), constraints (boundaries on length/tone/content), and format (the actual shape of the output). Most disappointing outputs trace back to a missing pillar, not a "dumb" model.
  • Specific verbs over vague verbs. "Summarize," "compare," "classify" describe a deliverable; "discuss," "cover," "talk about" describe an activity.
  • The test for "is this prompt good enough": could a competent person, given only this prompt, produce something close to what you need without guessing at anything important?
  • More detail isn't always better. Once a prompt covers what's genuinely needed, additional padding can clutter focus rather than improve output — and a long prompt can still be missing the one detail that actually matters.
