Interview Questions: Prompting Techniques I

Prompt engineering is consistently described by hiring managers as the single most "immediately testable" GenAI skill — interviewers often ask candidates to write or critique an actual prompt live, not just describe techniques abstractly. Expect to be asked to do zero-shot/few-shot/role prompting, not just define it. Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 Explain the difference between zero-shot, few-shot, and role prompting, with a one-line example of each.
Strong answer

Zero-shot = no examples given, just the instruction (e.g. "classify this review as positive/negative/neutral"). Few-shot = 2–5 example input/output pairs included in the prompt to demonstrate the pattern (e.g. worked support-ticket classifications before the real ticket). Role prompting = assigning the model a persona or expertise frame ("You are a senior tax accountant...") that shifts style and focus. A strong answer gives a quick concrete example of each rather than just reciting definitions, and ideally notes they can be combined.

Red flags

Confusing "role prompting" with "few-shot" (they're independent axes), or being unable to produce a concrete one-line example for at least two of the three on the spot.

Follow-up

"Write me a prompt that uses all three at once." (e.g. a role-assigned, few-shot classification prompt for a specific task.)

2 When would you use few-shot prompting instead of just writing clearer instructions?
Strong answer

Few-shot is especially useful when the desired output format, category scheme, or style is hard to describe precisely in words but easy to demonstrate — a very specific JSON structure, a particular tone, or a classification scheme with boundary cases that are easier to show than explain (like a company-specific "P0/P1/P2/P3" priority scheme). If the task is common-sense and the categories are unambiguous, clearer instructions alone (zero-shot) are usually enough and cheaper in tokens.

Red flags

Treating few-shot as strictly "better" and defaulting to it for every task regardless of whether the categories are already unambiguous — that just burns context window for no real gain.

Follow-up

"How many examples would you start with, and how would you choose them?" (Typically 2–5, covering the real range of cases including at least one boundary case — not several near-duplicates of the same case.)

3 Does role prompting actually give a model new expertise? Explain what it does and doesn't change.
Strong answer

No. Role prompting shifts style, vocabulary, and focus by drawing on patterns associated with that expert framing in the training data — it does not add new factual knowledge or guarantee accuracy the model didn't already have access to. Telling a model "you are a board-certified cardiologist" changes how it presents whatever it already knows; it doesn't grant medical expertise it lacked before. This matters for high-stakes use cases: role prompting is a real tool for tone and focus, not a substitute for verification or grounding in actual facts.

Red flags

Claiming a role assignment makes outputs "more accurate" or "more reliable" — that conflates a confident expert voice with verified expert accuracy, which is exactly the trap this question is testing for.

Follow-up

"If role prompting doesn't add knowledge, why use it at all?" (e.g. it's genuinely useful for shaping tone, vocabulary, and which aspects of a response get emphasized — a security-focused code review vs. a style-focused one.)

4 How would you design few-shot examples for a classification task with a tricky "none of the above" category?
Strong answer

Include at least one explicit worked example that lands in the "none of the above" / "Other" bucket — not just the named categories. Without it, the model has never seen what an unrelated input should map to, and tends to force genuinely off-topic inputs into whichever named category feels closest, simply because no other option was demonstrated. A strong answer also notes that a good "Other" example should be genuinely unrelated to the real categories, not just a weak/ambiguous version of one of them, so it teaches a clean boundary.

Red flags

Only listing examples for the "real" categories and treating "Other" as something the model should just infer on its own.

Follow-up

"How would you know if your 'Other' example wasn't enough?" (e.g. test against new, genuinely off-topic inputs the examples didn't cover, and see if any get force-fit into a real category.)

5 A teammate says: "I told the model it's a doctor, so now its medical answers must be accurate." What's the flaw in this reasoning?
Strong answer

The flaw: assigning a role changes the STYLE and FRAMING of the model's answers — drawing on patterns associated with medical-expert language in training data — but it does not grant verified medical knowledge or guarantee factual accuracy. A confident, doctor-sounding voice is not the same as verified expert accuracy. This connects directly to the fluency-versus-correctness lesson from earlier in the program: the model's tone was never a reliable signal of whether its content is actually right, and a role assignment only strengthens the tone, not the underlying accuracy.

Red flags

Agreeing with the teammate, or hedging without clearly stating that role prompting has no effect on factual accuracy.

Follow-up

"What would you actually do to increase confidence in a medical answer's accuracy?" (e.g. ground it in retrieved, verified sources rather than relying on persona framing alone — the seed of retrieval-augmented approaches.)

Strategy Tips

  • Practice the live-prompt-rewrite format specifically — interviewers often hand you a real (or vague) prompt and ask you to improve it out loud, explaining each change. This is the most common live-coding-equivalent for prompt engineering roles.
  • Cover the "Strong answer" line, answer out loud, then check yourself against it — that's how these are meant to be used for self-testing.
  • Anchor every technique to a concrete example you can produce on the spot — interviewers are listening for trade-off reasoning and real examples, not textbook recall.
