Prompting Techniques I — Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, Role Prompting

Session 2.1 gave you a framework for diagnosing a weak prompt. This chapter gives you three named tools for actively shaping how the model approaches a task — the exact vocabulary you'll hear in every real conversation about prompting from here on.

On this page 1. Zero-Shot Prompting — Just the Instruction 2. Few-Shot Prompting — Show, Don't Just Tell 3. Role Prompting — Assigning a Persona or Expertise Frame 4. Combining the Techniques
Here's a scene that plays out constantly in real teams: someone writes a prompt, it works fine on the three examples they tried, they ship it — and then it quietly misclassifies a chunk of real traffic because the model never saw an example of the case that broke it. The fix almost never requires a fancier model or a longer instruction. It requires knowing which of three specific techniques the situation actually calls for. Zero-shot, few-shot, and role prompting aren't competing philosophies you pick a favorite from — they're distinct tools that solve distinct problems, and knowing which one fits which job is the actual skill this chapter builds.
Sub-topic 1 of 4

1. Zero-Shot Prompting — Just the Instruction

Almost every prompt you've written so far in this program — including the worked rewrite from Session 2.1 — has been zero-shot, even if nobody called it that. It's the default mode of prompting, and for a lot of tasks, it's also the correct one.

What is... zero-shot prompting? Zero-shot prompting means giving the model an instruction with no examples of what the output should look like — just the task itself, relying on the model's general training to produce something reasonable. You're trusting that the model's training already encodes a strong enough understanding of the task to apply it correctly, with no demonstration required.
zero-shot-example.txt 
Classify this customer review as positive, negative, or neutral:
"The shipping took forever but the product itself works great."

Notice what's missing on purpose: not a single example of what a "positive" or "negative" classification looks like. For well-known, common-sense categories like these, that's fine — the model's training already contains an enormous amount of text where reviews get described exactly this way. Zero-shot works well when the task is conceptually clear from the instruction alone, the categories or format you want are common and unambiguous, and there isn't a subtle, hard-to-describe pattern you need the model to pick up on. Most everyday tasks — summarization, straightforward classification, translation, simple drafting — work fine zero-shot, especially once you've applied Session 2.1's four pillars (clarity, context, constraints, format) properly.

Where zero-shot starts to struggle Zero-shot tends to break down in three situations: when the task has a specific, non-obvious format you need followed exactly; when the categories or rules are unusual or company-specific — not something the model would have a strong default sense of; or when you need consistent edge-case handling that's hard to describe in words but easy to demonstrate. That gap — "I can't fully describe this pattern, but I can show it" — is exactly what the next technique exists to fill.
Quick recap
  • Zero-shot = instruction only, no examples. It's the default and the right first choice.
  • It works best for clear, common-sense tasks with unambiguous categories or formats.
  • It struggles on unusual/company-specific rules and hard-to-describe edge cases — the gap few-shot fills.

2. Few-Shot Prompting — Show, Don't Just Tell

What is... few-shot prompting? Few-shot prompting means including a small number of example input/output pairs within the prompt itself, demonstrating the exact pattern you want the model to follow, before giving it the real input to handle. The model isn't being "trained" in any permanent sense — these examples live only in this one request's context window (recall Session 1.2) — but they powerfully steer what a good response looks like for this specific call.

Suppose you're classifying support tickets into categories specific to your company — categories a general model wouldn't have any standard sense of. Zero-shot might fail here, because "Billing" vs. "Account Access" vs. "Other" isn't a universal concept the model already understands consistently for your exact product.

few-shot-tickets.txt 
Classify the following support ticket into one of: Billing, Technical, Account Access, Other.

Ticket: "I was charged twice for my subscription this month."
Category: Billing

Ticket: "The app crashes every time I try to upload a photo."
Category: Technical

Ticket: "I can't log in, it says my password is wrong even after I reset it."
Category: Account Access

Ticket: "Can you tell me when paddle boarding season starts?"
Category: Other

Ticket: "My invoice shows a fee I don't recognize from last week."
Category:

Notice what those four examples accomplish: they don't just state the categories (a zero-shot prompt could do that too) — they demonstrate the boundary cases. The model can now infer that "Billing" means money/charges-related, "Technical" means app malfunction, "Account Access" means login/credential issues, and crucially, that genuinely unrelated questions get "Other" rather than being forced into one of the real categories. That last example is doing real work: it's teaching the model what doesn't belong, which is often harder to convey through instruction alone.

Why this works: connecting back to Session 1.2 Recall that an LLM predicts the next token based on patterns in everything currently in its context window. Few-shot examples are a direct, powerful way to shape that context — instead of describing a pattern abstractly, you're handing the model several worked instances of the pattern, and it continues that pattern. It's the same fundamental mechanism from Sessions 1.1 and 1.2, just deliberately engineered.
How many examples is "few"
  • Typically 2–5 examples is enough to establish a clear pattern — more isn't automatically better.
  • Past a certain point you're consuming context window space (Session 1.2's "lost in the middle" risk applies here too) without meaningfully improving pattern recognition.
  • Choose examples that cover the real range of cases you expect, including at least one boundary or edge case if one exists — examples too similar to each other don't teach the model much beyond the first one.
Quick recap
  • Few-shot = 2–5 example input/output pairs embedded directly in the prompt, demonstrating the pattern.
  • It's especially powerful for company-specific categories, unusual formats, and boundary cases hard to describe in words.
  • Redundant, near-duplicate examples waste context without teaching anything new — diversity of examples matters more than count.

3. Role Prompting — Assigning a Persona or Expertise Frame

What is... role prompting? Role prompting means instructing the model to adopt a specific persona, professional identity, or expertise frame — "You are a senior tax accountant," "You are a patient, encouraging math tutor," "You are a skeptical code reviewer looking for security vulnerabilities." This shapes the style, vocabulary, priorities, and sometimes the focus of the response, without necessarily changing the underlying task instruction.

Mechanically, a role assignment shifts what's most statistically plausible to generate next, because "tax accountant" language patterns and "math tutor" language patterns look meaningfully different in the training data the model learned from. Assigning a role is, in effect, telling the model which region of its learned patterns to draw from — similar in spirit to how a system prompt lets you set persistent behavioral instructions, but specifically focused on identity and expertise framing rather than task mechanics.

before-after-role.txt 
WITHOUT role prompting:
"Review this code for issues."
  -> a generic, broad review touching on style, naming, maybe a few bugs

WITH role prompting:
"You are a security-focused code reviewer. Review this code specifically
for injection vulnerabilities, unsafe input handling, and authentication
weaknesses. Ignore style issues entirely."
  -> a narrower, more specialized review -- the role plus explicit scope
     steers attention toward a specific lens rather than a generic pass
The honest limit of role prompting
  • Role prompting shapes style and focus — it does not grant the model genuinely new capabilities it didn't already have.
  • Telling a model "you are a board-certified cardiologist" doesn't give it medical knowledge it lacked before; it shifts how it presents and frames whatever it already knows.
  • This matters enormously for anything high-stakes: role prompting is genuinely useful for tone, focus, and framing, but it is not a substitute for actual verification, actual domain expertise, or actual grounding in verified facts.
  • Don't mistake a confident expert voice for verified expert accuracy — the same fluency-vs-correctness lesson from Session 1.5, showing up again in a new costume.
Quick recap
  • Role prompting assigns a persona or expertise frame, shifting style, vocabulary, and focus.
  • It works by drawing on patterns associated with that expert voice in the training data — not by adding new knowledge.
  • A confident expert voice is not the same as verified expert accuracy — treat it as a framing tool, not a fact source.

4. Combining the Techniques

These three techniques aren't mutually exclusive — real prompts often combine them. A role-prompted, few-shot classification task is entirely reasonable, and often stronger than either technique used alone.

combined-example.txt 
You are a meticulous content moderator for a children's education platform.

Classify each comment as Approve or Flag for review.

Comment: "This video really helped me understand fractions!"
Classification: Approve

Comment: "lol this is so easy a baby could do it, what a waste of time"
Classification: Flag

Comment: "Can someone explain the third example again?"
Classification:

Here, the role ("meticulous content moderator for a children's platform") sets the lens and sensitivity level, while the few-shot examples demonstrate exactly where the line falls between borderline-rude-but-fine and actually-flag-worthy. Neither technique alone would do as much work as the combination: the role alone doesn't tell the model exactly where to draw the line, and the examples alone don't tell it how strict or lenient to be in the gray area.

Thought process: the real skill here isn't typing out the examples — it's deciding, before you write a single line, which technique the situation actually calls for. Before asking an AI assistant to help build a classifier like this, an experienced practitioner asks three questions in order: is this task common-sense enough for zero-shot? Does it have company-specific categories or boundary cases that need demonstrating (few-shot)? Does it need a specific lens, tone, or sensitivity level applied on top (role prompting)? The prompt you write should make all three decisions explicit, not leave them implicit.

Show a strong version of this prompt 
Write a Python function called classify_comment(client, comment) that sends a role-prompted, few-shot classification request to an LLM API. The prompt template should: (1) open with a role assignment appropriate to the domain -- "You are a meticulous content moderator for a children's education platform" -- (2) state the task and the exact two allowed labels, Approve or Flag, (3) include 2-4 worked examples as "Comment: ... / Classification: ..." pairs, with at least one clearly-fine example and one clearly-flag-worthy example, and (4) end with the real comment and a bare "Classification:" line for the model to complete. Keep the function's max_tokens small since the output is just one word.
Quick recap
  • Zero-shot, few-shot, and role prompting stack — combining them is often stronger than any one alone.
  • The role sets the lens/sensitivity; the examples set the exact boundary; neither substitutes for the other.
  • The real skill is deciding, before writing the prompt, which combination the task actually needs.
In the Real World
  • Zero-shot, few-shot, and role prompting are the three terms you'll hear most in any real conversation about prompting — in interviews, in documentation, among practitioners — precisely because they map onto three genuinely distinct problems.
  • A prompt that "works in testing" but misbehaves in production is very often a few-shot coverage gap: the test examples were too narrow a slice of what real traffic actually looks like.
  • Role prompting is a favorite lever for product teams shaping tone and focus (a "friendly onboarding assistant" vs. a "terse technical support bot") — but teams that lean on it for accuracy, not just tone, end up shipping confidently-wrong output.

Points to Remember

  • Zero-shot: instruction only, no examples. The right default for clear, common-sense tasks.
  • Few-shot: 2–5 example input/output pairs included in the prompt, demonstrating the exact pattern — especially powerful for company-specific categories, unusual formats, or conveying boundary cases that are hard to describe in words.
  • Role prompting: assigning a persona or expertise frame, which shifts style, vocabulary, and focus — but does NOT grant new capabilities the model didn't already have. A confident expert voice is not the same as verified expert accuracy.
  • These techniques combine. A role-prompted few-shot prompt is often stronger than either technique used alone.
  • More few-shot examples isn't automatically better — 2–5 well-chosen examples, including boundary cases, typically outperforms a longer list of similar ones.
