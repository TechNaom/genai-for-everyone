Session 2.3 Interview Prep 10 min read

Interview Questions: Chain-of-Thought, Step-Back, Self-Consistency

Prompt engineering is consistently described by hiring managers as the single most "immediately testable" GenAI skill — interviewers often ask candidates to write or critique an actual prompt live, not just define techniques abstractly. These questions reflect that pattern. Each one includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 What is chain-of-thought prompting, and what's a risk of relying on it too heavily?
Strong answer

Asking the model to reason step-by-step before giving a final answer, which often improves accuracy on multi-step problems because each generated reasoning step becomes part of the context that later steps are predicted from. The risk: a confident, well-structured chain of reasoning can still arrive at a wrong conclusion, so the reasoning text itself is not proof of correctness — this connects directly back to Week 1's core warning that fluency and accuracy are separate properties.

Red flags

Describing chain-of-thought as something that "makes the model actually think," or claiming that visible reasoning guarantees a correct answer. Also weak: no mention of the mechanism (why it helps) at all, just "it makes answers better."

Follow-up

"How would you actually verify a chain-of-thought answer before shipping it, rather than just reading it and feeling satisfied?" (e.g. checking intermediate steps against known facts/math, or running self-consistency for a second signal.)

2 When would step-back prompting help more than just asking the question directly?
Strong answer

When the question has a real underlying decision framework or general principle worth surfacing first — comparisons, recommendations, and "should we do X or Y" questions especially. Step-back asks the model to articulate that general framework before applying it to the specific case, which tends to produce a more grounded, better-justified answer than jumping straight to a recommendation. It's not worth the extra step for simple factual lookups, where there's no meaningful "general principle" layer to surface.

Red flags

Applying step-back to every question regardless of whether a framework actually exists, or being unable to give a concrete example of a question type where it helps versus one where it doesn't.

Follow-up

"Give me an example of a question where step-back would just add noise." (e.g. "what's the capital of France" — there's no general framework to surface before answering a simple lookup.)

3 What is self-consistency prompting, and when is it worth the extra cost?
Strong answer

Running the same prompt multiple times, often with sampling randomness enabled, and comparing the results — taking the most common answer or examining where runs agree and disagree, rather than trusting a single response. It's worth it for genuinely high-stakes or ambiguous cases, where being wrong is costly enough to justify multiplying token cost and latency by the number of runs. It's not a default you'd apply to every request in a production system — a strong answer explicitly ties this to a cost/speed trade-off, not just "more calls = more confidence."

Red flags

Treating self-consistency as free, or not being able to name what convergence vs. divergence across runs actually signals (uncertainty on the model's part, not a guarantee either way).

Follow-up

"If five runs give you five different answers, what do you actually do next?" (e.g. don't pick one arbitrarily — treat it as a signal the case needs human review or a different approach, not a tiebreaker to resolve on your own.)

4 How would you decide which prompting technique to apply to a new task you've never seen before?
Strong answer

Start by identifying the specific failure mode you're worried about, not by reaching for the "fanciest" technique. A simple factual question needs nothing beyond zero-shot. A company-specific format or classification pattern benefits from few-shot. A task with several dependent calculation or logic steps benefits from chain-of-thought. A comparison or recommendation with a real underlying framework benefits from step-back. A genuinely ambiguous, high-stakes decision justifies the added cost of self-consistency. The strongest answers explicitly say techniques should be combined only when each one is solving a distinct problem (e.g. role prompting + few-shot), not stacked reflexively.

Red flags

"I'd just add chain-of-thought to everything to be safe" — this ignores the real cost/complexity trade-off and shows no judgment about when a technique is unnecessary overhead.

Follow-up

"Walk me through your decision for this task: summarizing a single support ticket into one sentence." (Expected: zero-shot or maybe a light format instruction — no need for chain-of-thought, step-back, or self-consistency on something this simple and low-stakes.)

5 A teammate shows you a beautifully formatted, step-by-step chain-of-thought response and says "it must be right, look how thorough it is." How do you respond?
Strong answer

Push back gently but clearly: thoroughness and formatting quality are properties of the presentation, not proof of correctness. A wrong answer can be produced just as confidently and step-by-step as a correct one — the model doesn't have an internal "I'm not sure about this step" signal that shows up as sloppier formatting. The response should actually recommend verifying the reasoning (checking intermediate steps against known facts or a calculation), especially if the decision is at all consequential, rather than trusting it because it looks rigorous.

Red flags

Agreeing that thorough formatting is itself evidence of correctness, or not offering any concrete next step (just saying "you should always double check things" with no method).

Follow-up

"What's a fast, practical way to sanity-check a chain-of-thought answer without redoing the whole thing by hand?" (e.g. spot-check just the highest-risk intermediate step, or run self-consistency for a quick convergence signal.)

Strategy Tips

  • Cover the "Strong answer" line, answer out loud, then check yourself against it — that's how these are meant to be used for self-testing.
  • Interviewers are listening for trade-off reasoning and concrete examples, not textbook recall. When in doubt, anchor every technique to a task where it clearly helps and one where it would be overkill.
  • Practice narrating a live technique-selection decision out loud (question 4's format) — it's the closest analog to a real live-prompting interview round.
