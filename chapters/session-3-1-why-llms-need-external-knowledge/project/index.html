Session 3.1 Project 20 min build

The Wrong-Direction Failure Report

The Pro path build for this session — built directly on the "Pro path" prompt at the bottom of the lesson: for each of the six scenarios from the exercises worksheet, beyond just naming the right call, sketch what actually goes wrong when someone makes the call in the opposite direction — a full RAG pipeline for a scenario that never needed one, or prompting alone for a scenario that genuinely needed retrieval.

What to build

The same six scenarios from the exercises worksheet, each already tagged with its correct_call ("RAG" or "Not RAG") and its wrong_direction — the specific opposite-direction mistake to analyze. Scenarios 1, 3, 4, and 6 genuinely need RAG, so their wrong-direction mistake is handling them with prompting alone. Scenarios 2 and 5 don't need RAG at all, so their wrong-direction mistake is building a full retrieval pipeline anyway. Fill in the three None fields for each case:

  • wrong_call — one sentence on what the opposite-direction mistake looks like in practice.
  • failure_mode — 2–3 sentences on what concretely breaks: who notices, how, and why it's bad.
  • cost_of_the_mistake — one sentence naming the type of cost (wasted engineering time, latency, a wrong answer shipped to a real user, a compliance risk).
The habit this trains It's easy to memorize which scenarios need RAG as a checklist. It's far more valuable to be able to explain the cost of getting it wrong in either direction — skipping retrieval on a genuine RAG scenario ships a confidently wrong answer to a real user, while building retrieval infrastructure for a scenario that never needed it burns engineering time and latency for no accuracy gain. Naming the specific failure, not just "that's the wrong call," is real engineering judgment.

Ideas to make it your own

  • Pick one scenario and sketch what the fix would look like in code or pseudocode once the wrong call has already shipped — how would you detect it in production before a customer does?
  • Write a seventh scenario from your own work or coursework where you've seen (or could imagine) someone reach for RAG when they didn't need it, or skip it when they did — and analyze it the same way.

Why this project matters

Session 3.1's real lesson isn't "here's what RAG is" — it's "here's how to tell when you need it." That judgment only sharpens once you can articulate the cost of getting it wrong in both directions, not just recite the right answer. An engineer who can say "skipping retrieval here means a support bot confidently misstates a return policy to a real customer" or "adding retrieval here means burning latency and engineering time on a task the model already does well" is demonstrating exactly the judgment real GenAI work demands — and exactly what the rest of Week 3's engineering will assume you already have.
