Interview Questions: Vector Databases & Retrieval

Chunking strategy and vector database selection are two of the most reliably asked questions once an interview moves past embeddings 101 and into "how would you actually build this." Interviewers are listening for trade-off reasoning grounded in real numbers (scale, latency, cost), not a memorized list of product names. Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 You're building a RAG system over a 500-page legal document. How would you chunk it, and what trade-offs would you consider?
Strong answer

Names the core trade-off directly: small chunks retrieve precisely but risk losing surrounding context; large chunks preserve context but dilute the specific signal and can retrieve irrelevant material alongside the useful part. Mentions overlap as a way to avoid orphaning ideas at a chunk boundary. Crucially, ties the chunking strategy to the document type — legal text is dense and structured (sections, clauses, definitions), so a strong answer respects those natural boundaries rather than cutting at a rigid word count that might land mid-clause. Bonus: mentions storing metadata (section number, page, document title) alongside each chunk for traceability.

Red flags

"Just split every 512 tokens" with no acknowledgment of trade-offs, no mention of document structure, or no awareness of why overlap matters at all.

Follow-up

"Your retrieval keeps returning irrelevant sections for a specific clause lookup. What's your first hypothesis, and how would you test it?" (e.g. chunk size may be too large and diluting the specific clause with unrelated surrounding text — test by re-chunking smaller and re-running the same query.)

2 You're choosing a vector database for a production RAG system handling 10 million documents. What factors would you evaluate, and would you pick an open-source or managed solution?
Strong answer

Lays out concrete evaluation criteria beyond "which one is popular": scale (does it actually handle 10M+ vectors with acceptable performance), query latency, concurrent query throughput, cost model (per-query vs. per-month), durability (backups, replication), and how easily vectors can be updated or re-embedded in place as documents change. On open-source vs. managed, names the real trade-off: open-source (e.g. FAISS, Milvus) gives more control but adds operational burden; managed (e.g. Pinecone, Weaviate Cloud) reduces ops work but introduces vendor lock-in and typically higher per-query cost. A strong answer treats the choice as depending on team size and ops maturity, not a universal "best" tool.

Red flags

"Just pick Pinecone" (or any single tool) with no reasoning attached; no mention of latency or throughput; no cost consideration; treating "good at scale" and "production-ready" as the same thing.

Follow-up

"Query latency is creeping up to 500ms in production. What are your options?" (e.g. tune the ANN index's accuracy/speed knob, shard the index, cache frequent queries, or reduce k.)

3 Why can't you just embed an entire document as a single vector for retrieval purposes?
Strong answer

A single embedding for a long document has to represent everything in it at once, so it becomes an averaged, blurred representation of the whole thing. A specific query about one narrow part of the document then has to match against this single vague vector instead of a vector that actually represents the relevant passage — which produces poor, imprecise retrieval. A strong answer connects this directly to why chunking exists at all: it's the fix for exactly this problem.

Red flags

Answering with something about computation time or storage limits instead of the actual semantic problem (the blurred/averaged vector), or implying there's a hard length cutoff on what can technically be embedded.

Follow-up

"What's the failure mode at the opposite extreme — chunking down to individual sentences?" (e.g. context collapse: a sentence that was clear in its paragraph becomes nearly meaningless once isolated.)

4 Walk me through the trade-off in choosing top-k for a retrieval system. What happens at the extremes?
Strong answer

Names both failure directions with a concrete mechanism for each: too low a k (e.g. k=1) risks missing the answer if it's split across adjacent chunks, or if the single top-similarity chunk isn't actually the most useful one — with no fallback candidate. Too high a k (e.g. k=50) dilutes the context handed to the model with mostly-irrelevant text, raises cost and latency, and can measurably hurt answer quality because the model has to sift through noise to find the signal. A strong answer names a realistic starting range (roughly k=3–10) and frames the exact value as something you tune against real queries, not something you can derive in the abstract.

Red flags

Only describing one side of the trade-off, or suggesting there's a single objectively correct k independent of the documents and query style.

Follow-up

"You've re-ranked the top-k candidates and precision improved, but recall dropped. Why, and how do you balance the two?" (e.g. re-ranking narrows toward the candidates that look most precisely relevant, which can push out a correct-but-less-obvious match that a looser first pass would have kept — the fix is usually widening the initial retrieval set before re-ranking, not narrowing k earlier in the pipeline.)

5 Give an example of a chunk that would be semantically close to a query but still not useful for answering it.
Strong answer

A concrete example works best: an introductory paragraph that briefly name-drops every topic in a document ("this handbook covers leave, remote work, expenses, and benefits") will score moderately well against almost any query in that document, because it shares at least a little vocabulary with every topic — without substantively answering any specific one. A strong answer names why this matters in production: it's a real, common failure mode where the system retrieves something plausible-looking rather than failing loudly and obviously, which makes it more dangerous, not less. Bonus: connects this to why re-ranking or hybrid keyword+semantic search can help filter it out.

Red flags

Unable to produce a concrete example, or conflating "semantically close" with "obviously wrong" — the whole point is that these chunks look reasonable at first glance.

Follow-up

"How would you detect this failure mode in production before a user complains?" (e.g. log retrieved chunks and scores, spot-check whether top-1 results are actually specific and substantive rather than generically topical, and build a small labeled test set of queries with known correct chunks to measure retrieval accuracy against.)

Strategy Tips

  • Cover the "Strong answer" line, answer out loud, then check yourself against it — that's how these are meant to be used for self-testing.
  • Interviewers are listening for trade-off reasoning grounded in real numbers, not a list of vector database names. Always attach a concrete "why" to any tool you mention.
  • If you've actually run the chunk-size comparison from this session's exercise, use your own retrieved results as your example — a real, specific observation beats a generic textbook answer every time.
