Interview Questions: RAG Failure Modes and Debugging

RAG debugging questions show up constantly in GenAI/ML engineering interviews in 2026, because they separate candidates who've actually run a retrieval pipeline from candidates who can only describe one in the abstract. Interviewers are listening for a structured debugging process — isolate retrieval from generation, inspect real evidence, name the specific failure — not a vague "just tune it until it works." Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 A RAG system gives a wrong answer. Walk me through exactly what you'd check first, second, and third.
Strong answer

First: inspect the actual retrieved chunks and their similarity scores for that exact query — not the final answer's wording. Second: check whether the correct chunk is present at all in the top-k (a miss) or present but buried/diluted among many weak results (stuffing), or check whether it's split awkwardly across a chunk boundary (a chunking error). Third, and only after retrieval is confirmed clean, look at the generation step itself — is the model actually using the context it was given, or ignoring it? Testing retrieval in isolation (without the LLM in the loop) is what makes this a structured process instead of a guess.

Red flags

Jumping straight to "the model is bad at following instructions" or "just retrain it" without ever looking at what was retrieved. No mention of isolating retrieval from generation.

Follow-up

"The correct chunk WAS retrieved and WAS in the top-3. What do you check next?" (Now it's a generation-side problem — check the prompt template, whether instructions to cite only retrieved context are being followed, and whether the context was truncated or reordered before reaching the model.)

2 Explain why "just increase k" can sometimes make a RAG system worse, not better.
Strong answer

Raising k retrieves more chunks unconditionally — it doesn't filter for whether those additional chunks are actually relevant. If similarity scores drop off sharply after the first few results (a common pattern), a higher k mostly adds low-relevance or near-zero-relevance chunks that dilute the genuinely useful ones, which is context stuffing. It only reliably helps when the failure was specifically a near-miss sitting just outside a narrow cutoff — and even then, a targeted fix like re-ranking usually beats blindly raising k for everyone.

Red flags

Treating "more context" as universally safe or helpful. No mention of a relevance cliff or diminishing signal quality as k grows.

Follow-up

"How would you decide the right k for a given corpus, concretely?" (e.g. plot similarity score vs. rank across a representative set of test queries and look for where the curve drops off sharply.)

3 How does re-ranking address both retrieval misses and context stuffing with a single mechanism?
Strong answer

Re-ranking is a two-pass design: a wide, cheap first pass (e.g. top-20 by cosine similarity) followed by a narrower, more expensive second pass that directly scores query-vs-candidate relevance for just those 20. It helps misses because a near-miss chunk that scored just below a narrow top-3 cutoff still survives into the wider top-20 pool and gets a second, more careful look. It helps stuffing because, even though the initial net is wide, only the re-ranked best few actually reach the generation step — you get the safety net of a wide search without paying the dilution cost of handing all 20 to the model.

Red flags

Describing re-ranking as just "retrieving more" without the two-pass structure, or missing that the second pass uses a genuinely different, more relevance-aware comparison than plain cosine similarity.

Follow-up

"Would re-ranking fix a miss caused by a query using 'telecommute' when the source only says 'remote work'?" (Not reliably on its own — that's closer to zero vocabulary overlap under a lexical embedding, not a near-miss; a re-ranker needs semantic, not just lexical, relevance scoring, or the underlying embedding model itself needs upgrading.)

4 What's the difference between a pipeline failure and a trust/evaluation failure in a RAG system, and why does the distinction matter?
Strong answer

A pipeline failure (chunking error, retrieval miss, context stuffing) has a discoverable, specific cause inside a specific stage, and fixing that stage fixes the symptom. A trust/evaluation failure can happen even when chunking, retrieval, and context assembly all technically worked correctly — the answer is still subtly wrong in a way no single component explains, and catching it requires systematic evaluation (e.g. a labeled test set, automated scoring, human review) rather than a config change. The distinction matters because misdiagnosing one as the other wastes effort: tuning chunk size won't fix a genuine evaluation gap, and building an eval harness won't fix a chunk boundary cutting through a key sentence.

Red flags

Treating every wrong answer as the same undifferentiated "the AI was wrong" bucket, with no attempt to separate a fixable pipeline stage from a systemic trust problem.

Follow-up

"Give a concrete example of a RAG answer that's wrong even though retrieval and chunking are both fine." (e.g. the model retrieves the right two policy chunks but merges them incorrectly, stating a rule that's true of neither policy individually — a generation-level trust problem, not a pipeline problem.)

5 You deployed a RAG system and it's returning irrelevant documents while the LLM hallucinates facts not in the retrieved context. Walk me through how you'd debug and fix it.
Strong answer

First isolate the two problems, because they're diagnosed differently: log the retrieved documents and scores to check whether retrieval itself is returning relevant material (is top-1 actually relevant to the query?), and separately test retrieval alone, without the LLM in the loop, to confirm whether the irrelevance is a retrieval problem or a chunking problem (losing context at boundaries). For the hallucination specifically — facts appearing that aren't even in the retrieved context — the fix is usually generation-side: tighten the prompt to explicitly instruct "only use facts from the provided context" and add citation grounding, requiring the model to cite which retrieved chunk supports each claim, which makes ungrounded claims visibly detectable.

Red flags

"Just increase chunk size" with no structured debugging. Not isolating retrieval from generation. No mention of concrete metrics (e.g. top-1 relevance, or checking scores directly). Jumping to "retrain the model" without diagnosing which stage actually failed.

Follow-up

"You re-ranked and it helped, but recall dropped. Why, and how do you balance precision vs. recall?" (Re-ranking trades some recall for precision by discarding lower-scored candidates from the wider pool; the balance depends on whether missing a relevant chunk or including an irrelevant one is more costly for the specific use case — often tuned by adjusting how many candidates survive the re-ranking cut.)

Strategy Tips

  • Cover the "Strong answer" line, answer out loud, then check yourself against it — that's how these are meant to be used for self-testing.
  • Interviewers are listening for a structured debugging process, not the "right" final word — narrate the order you'd check things in, the way this session's worked example did.
  • If you've actually run broken_pipeline.py from this session's exercise, reference it: being able to say "I've diagnosed exactly this kind of vocabulary-mismatch miss before, here's what the similarity scores looked like" is a far stronger signal than reciting the definition.
