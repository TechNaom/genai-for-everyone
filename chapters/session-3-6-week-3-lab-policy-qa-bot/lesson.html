Week 3 Lab — Campus Student Services Q&A Bot

Five sessions of retrieval theory, one genuinely messy set of real documents. Today is where "it worked on the demo doc" meets four PDFs that don't agree on how to organize themselves.

On this page 1. The build: a bot over four cross-referencing PDFs 2. Why this build needs every technique from the week 3. The bug you're going to find, and the fix 4. "Done well" vs. "done technically" 5. Reflection: what clicked, what's fuzzy
Picture a college sophomore, mid-semester, who just dropped a class and fell below full-time enrollment. She lives in a dorm. Does that put her housing at risk? Does it affect her financial aid for next semester? She doesn't know which office to call, doesn't know which of four different PDFs scattered across the university's website actually has the answer, and the help desk is juggling forty other students with forty other questions. This is the scenario behind today's lab — and it's also, not coincidentally, close to the single most common real-world reason organizations build a RAG system in the first place: a question with a real, document-backed answer, but one that's buried across more than one document, written by more than one office, on more than one schedule. Today you build the system that finds it — and you find, and fix, a real retrieval bug along the way.
Sub-topic 1 of 5

1. The build: a bot over four cross-referencing PDFs

You're building a Q&A bot that answers student questions by retrieving from four separate campus documents instead of one tidy handbook: a Registration Guide, a Financial Aid Handbook, an Academic Standing Policy, and a Housing Handbook. Sessions 3.3 and 3.4 deliberately used a single, self-contained document to teach chunking and RAG cleanly. Today does something different on purpose.

These four documents were clearly written by different offices, at different times, and they genuinely cross-reference each other the way real institutional documents do. The Registration Guide mentions financial aid holds. The Housing Handbook mentions full-time enrollment status. The Academic Standing Policy mentions both financial aid suspension and course load limits. None of that overlap is an accident in the writing — the topics genuinely are connected in the real world, even though they live in separate files.

What is... multi-document retrieval risk? With one document, every retrieved chunk is at least from "the right place" by definition — the worst case is an imprecise match. With four overlapping documents, retrieval can confidently hand back a chunk from the wrong document entirely, and that chunk might even look like a strong match, because it touches the right topic in passing without actually containing the right answer. That's a failure mode a single-document system structurally cannot have.
Quick recap
  • Today's corpus is four real, cross-referencing campus PDFs, not one clean handbook.
  • Cross-referencing content is realistic, not an artificial complication — and it's exactly what makes multi-document retrieval harder.
  • The new failure mode: a confidently wrong document, not just an imprecise chunk.

Sub-topic 3 of 5

3. The bug you're going to find, and the fix

Here are the two questions that break things, and they break in the same way for a connected reason:

the-two-failing-questions.txt 
"What is the maximum course load for a student on probation?"
  -> real answer (13 credit hours) lives in academic_standing_policy.pdf

"What happens if I drop below full-time enrollment while living in a dorm?"
  -> real answer lives in housing_handbook.pdf

Both are clearly written, clearly labeled, easy for a human to find.
With blank-line chunking, BOTH questions instead retrieve registration_guide.pdf.

Why? The same upstream mechanism you may already be alert to from earlier weeks: PDF text extraction does not reliably preserve the blank lines that visually separated sections on the page. The Registration Guide has five sections — Add/Drop Deadlines, Course Load Requirements, Waitlists, Registration Holds, Transfer Credit — and when extracted as text, there's no blank line anywhere between them. A blank-line-based chunker finds exactly one giant "paragraph" for the entire document, and the whole ~240-word block becomes a single chunk. Inside it, words like "course load," "full-time," "financial aid," and "Academic Standing Policy" all appear together — not because that section is actually about probation or housing, but because policy writers naturally cross-reference related topics in passing. That cross-referencing language is exactly what makes the merged chunk score deceptively well against questions that are really about other documents.

Why the earlier fix doesn't just transfer over If you've worked with a different RAG corpus before, you might already know a fix for "blank lines don't survive PDF extraction": split on the document's own section headers instead. That instinct is correct — but notice it isn't a fact about that specific fix, it's a fact about needing a fix that matches your document's structure. This corpus's documents don't number their sections. They use headers written in ALL CAPS on their own line — "COURSE LOAD REQUIREMENTS," "ACADEMIC PROBATION," "HOUSING ELIGIBILITY AND COURSE LOAD." A splitting rule built for numbered headers would find nothing to split on here at all.

Try the core mechanic of both chunkers here — a small, self-contained version of the same regex logic chunk_text() and chunk_text_by_caps_header() use, run against a tiny stand-in for a merged, blank-line-free document:

chunking_bug.py
Click "Run Code" — notice the blank-line splitter finds exactly ONE chunk for a document with three obvious sections, while the caps-header splitter correctly finds three.

This is the real bug, shrunk down to a runnable size. On the actual corpus, that one merged chunk is what confidently outscores the correct document on both failing questions.

Thought process: the fix isn't "know the one true chunking regex" — it's the diagnostic sequence that gets you there. Print the chunks, notice there's suspiciously few of them, try a few different target_words values and notice the boundary never moves (proof the bug is upstream of chunk size), then print repr(text[:300]) and actually look at how the real extracted text is structured before writing a single line of new regex. That sequence transfers to any new document type; the specific regex does not.

Show a strong version of this prompt 
I have a RAG pipeline where blank-line-based paragraph chunking is merging an entire multi-section PDF into a single chunk, because the PDF's text extraction doesn't preserve blank lines between sections. The sections in this specific corpus use ALL-CAPS headers on their own line (e.g. "COURSE LOAD REQUIREMENTS"), not numbered headers. Write a Python function chunk_text_by_caps_header(text) that uses re.split with a lookahead to split the text right before any line that is entirely uppercase (allowing spaces and slashes), without consuming that header line, then strips whitespace and drops empty chunks. Also write a short comment explaining why this specific regex would NOT work on a document that uses numbered headers instead, to make clear the fix is corpus-specific.
Go Deeper: the transferable lesson isn't a regex

The lesson underneath today's lab, stated as plainly as possible: there is no universal chunking fix, only a universal chunking principle — look at how your real documents are actually structured, and split on whatever pattern reliably survives whatever transformation your text went through to reach you. For numbered documents, that's the numbering. For documents like these, written by people who format with capitalized headers instead, the fix is to split on lines that are entirely uppercase. The code is different. The principle — match your strategy to your actual documents, verified by actually running it, not assumed from a previous success — is identical.

Quick recap
  • Both failing questions retrieve registration_guide.pdf instead of the correct document, because blank-line chunking merges its five sections into one diluted chunk.
  • Changing target_words doesn't help — the bug is upstream of chunk size, in the paragraph split never finding more than one paragraph.
  • The fix splits on ALL-CAPS header lines, matching how this corpus is actually structured — a numbered-header fix would find nothing to split on here.
  • The transferable lesson is the diagnostic principle, not the specific regex.

4. "Done well" vs. "done technically"

It's possible to build something that runs without errors and still misses the point of the exercise. Here's the difference, concretely.

Done technically (passes, but misses the point) You see the Part 4 assertion fail, copy a caps-header regex from a search result or an assistant without checking it against repr(text[:300]) of your own extracted documents, and it happens to pass the test. It runs. It doesn't crash. But you can't explain why blank-line chunking failed here, or why the same fix wouldn't work on a differently-formatted corpus — which means the next time this bug shows up in a new shape, you're back to guessing.
Done well (the actual goal) You print the Registration Guide's chunks first and notice there's exactly one, suspiciously large. You try a few different target_words values and confirm the boundary never moves — ruling out chunk size as the cause. You print repr(text[:300]) and actually look at where the newlines are. Only then do you write chunk_text_by_caps_header(), because you've confirmed what pattern the real extracted text follows, not because you assumed it.

That distinction — diagnosing before fixing, on this specific corpus's actual extracted text, rather than pattern-matching to a fix that worked somewhere else — is exactly the Session 3.5 skill this lab is built to test under slightly harder, more realistic conditions.

Quick recap
  • "Passes the assertion" and "you can explain why it failed and why the fix works" are different bars.
  • Ruling out chunk size first (via target_words) is what proves the bug is upstream, in the paragraph split itself.
  • A fix copied without inspecting your own corpus's real extracted text is a coin flip on the next document.

5. Reflection: what clicked, what's fuzzy

Before moving into Week 4, take five honest minutes (genuinely worth doing, not a platitude) to answer these for yourself:

  • Could you explain, without looking back, why retrieval over multiple cross-referencing documents has a failure mode a single-document system structurally can't have? If shaky, revisit the opening of this session.
  • Could you sketch, from memory, the exact diagnostic sequence you'd run on a new, unfamiliar chunking bug — before writing any fix? If not, Session 3.5 is worth a second pass.
  • Did the caps-header bug surprise you anywhere — a place where you assumed a fix that worked before would just transfer, until you actually looked at this corpus's extracted text? That surprise is valuable information.

This isn't busywork. The single best predictor of struggling with real retrieval systems later is carrying forward the assumption that one chunking fix generalizes to every document you'll ever meet — because it doesn't, and every new document type is a fresh chance to find that out the expensive way instead of the five-minute way.

Quick recap
  • Honest self-assessment now is cheaper than discovering a gap later.
  • Shaky on multi-document retrieval risk → the opening of this session. Shaky on the diagnosis sequence → Session 3.5.
  • The surprises during the build are the most useful signal you have.
In the Real World
  • Nearly every real institutional or enterprise RAG system — HR policy bots, internal wikis, campus help desks, compliance assistants — deals with exactly this problem: several documents from several authors that genuinely overlap in subject matter, written on different timelines with different formatting conventions.
  • "It worked on the document I tested with" is one of the most common false-confidence traps in shipped RAG systems — a chunking strategy validated against one document's structure silently breaks the moment a new document with a different formatting convention is added to the corpus.
  • Source-document labeling on citations, the way this lab requires it, is standard practice in production RAG systems specifically because it turns a wrong-document retrieval into something a reviewer can catch in one glance, instead of something that only surfaces after a user reports a confusing or wrong answer.

Points to Remember

  • Multi-document RAG systems fail in a way single-document systems structurally can't: retrieval can confidently return the wrong document entirely, not just an imprecise chunk from the right one.
  • Real institutional documents genuinely cross-reference each other — and that overlap is exactly what makes a merged, overly broad chunk look deceptively relevant to questions it can't actually answer.
  • PDF text extraction unreliably preserves blank lines between sections, which can silently break blank-line-based chunking regardless of target_words — the failure is upstream of chunk size entirely.
  • A chunking fix that worked for one document's structure doesn't automatically transfer to a different document's structure. The universal lesson is a principle — inspect your real documents, split on whatever survives extraction intact, verify by running it — not a specific regex.
  • Source-document labels on every citation make a wrong-document retrieval visible at a glance, rather than something a reviewer has to manually dig for.
