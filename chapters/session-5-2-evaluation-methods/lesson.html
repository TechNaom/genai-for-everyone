Session 5.2 Intermediate 35–40 min read

Evaluation Methods

Session 5.1 gave you a golden dataset and a rubric. This chapter is about what happens when a rubric that works beautifully by hand can't possibly scale to 1,500 outputs — and why no single automated substitute is safe to trust alone.

On this page 1. Rubric Grading (Manual) 2. LLM-as-Judge (Automated) 3. Automatic Metrics 4. Human-in-the-Loop 5. Building an Evaluation Harness 6. Combining Methods — A Worked Comparison 7. Common Pitfalls
A legal tech startup builds a tool that reads contract clauses and produces a one-sentence, plain-English summary — meant for a non-lawyer to quickly understand what a clause actually commits them to. A summary like "You're giving up your right to sue in court and agreeing to arbitration instead" for an arbitration clause. The team needs to evaluate three different prompt variants before picking one to ship. But how do you score "is this summary faithful to the clause" at scale? You can't read all three variants' output on 500 clauses yourself — that's 1,500 summaries. You also can't just trust an automated score blindly, because a summary can use completely different words and still be faithful, or use very similar words and still misrepresent the clause's actual legal effect. This is exactly the problem Session 5.1's golden dataset and rubric don't fully solve on their own: a rubric is accurate but doesn't scale to 1,500 summaries by hand, and a single automated metric can be fooled. This chapter covers four evaluation methods, what each one is actually good at, and how to combine them so you're not relying on any single one's blind spots.
Sub-topic 1 of 7

1. Rubric Grading (Manual)

What is... rubric grading? A human scores each output against explicit criteria — the same approach from Session 5.1, just formalized here as one tool among several rather than the whole story.

Rubric grading's strengths are exactly what you'd expect from a careful human reader: it catches subtle legal misrepresentation a keyword match would miss, it can judge things a machine can't easily check — like whether a summary downplays how serious a clause actually is — and it's transparent, since you can explain exactly why a summary scored low. Here's a rubric shaped for this exact task:

rubric.py 
def grade_summary(clause_text, summary, expected_meaning):
    """Manual rubric grading -- same shape as Session 5.1's rubric"""

    # Legal Accuracy (0-2): does the summary correctly state what the
    # clause actually does, legally?
    accuracy = 2  # human judgment call

    # Completeness (0-2): does it omit a legally significant detail?
    # e.g., an arbitration clause that also waives class actions --
    # summarizing only "arbitration" and skipping "no class action"
    # is incomplete even if the arbitration part is correct
    completeness = 2

    # Plain Language (0-1): would a non-lawyer actually understand it?
    plain_language = 1

    # Severity Match (0-2): does the summary's TONE match how serious
    # the clause actually is? A summary of a binding arbitration clause
    # written in a breezy, low-stakes tone is a failure even if the
    # facts are technically correct
    severity_match = 2

    return accuracy + completeness + plain_language + severity_match  # /7

Notice the Severity Match criterion — it's the kind of thing no keyword-based check could ever catch. A summary can state every fact correctly and still fail if its tone makes a binding, consequential clause sound trivial. That's rubric grading's real advantage: it can weigh things a computation simply has no way to represent.

But the cost is real too. A lawyer reviewing summaries for legal accuracy might take 2–3 minutes each — and 1,500 summaries at 2 minutes each is 50 hours. Worse, a tired reviewer on summary #380 grades differently than on summary #12, even with the exact same rubric in front of them. Rubric grading doesn't scale, and it isn't even perfectly consistent with itself over a long session.

When to actually reach for it
  • The final, smaller-scale check before shipping — golden-dataset size, not the full test set.
  • Anywhere subjective legal or ethical judgment is the actual bottleneck, not a fact you could check computationally.
Quick recap
  • Rubric grading catches subtle misrepresentation and tone problems no automated check can, and it's fully transparent.
  • It's slow (50 hours for 1,500 summaries at 2–3 minutes each) and drifts as a reviewer tires over a long session.
  • Best used at golden-dataset scale as a final check, not as the only method across a full test set.

2. LLM-as-Judge (Automated)

What is... LLM-as-judge? Use an LLM to score the summaries automatically, instead of a human reading all 1,500.

This is fast — it scores 1,500 summaries in minutes, not 50 hours. It's consistent in the sense that it doesn't get "tired" the way a human reviewer does. And it can still make a genuine judgment call on something a rule-based metric can't, like tone. Here's an implementation for the contract summarizer:

llm_judge.py 
from anthropic import Anthropic

client = Anthropic()

def llm_score_summary(clause_text, summary):
    """Use an LLM to judge faithfulness of a contract clause summary"""

    prompt = f"""You are reviewing a plain-English summary of a legal
contract clause for accuracy.

Clause text:
{clause_text}

Summary given to the user:
{summary}

Score 0-10 on whether the summary is legally accurate AND captures
the full practical effect of the clause (not just part of it).
A summary that is technically true but misses a legally significant
detail (e.g., mentions arbitration but omits a class-action waiver)
should score no higher than 5.

Score (just the number):"""

    response = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022",
        max_tokens=10,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}]
    )

    return int(response.content[0].text.strip())

Look closely at that prompt's third paragraph — it's not decoration. Here's the concrete failure mode it exists to prevent: an LLM judge asked simply "is this summary accurate?" will often score a summary that mentions arbitration but completely omits a buried class-action waiver as an 8 or 9 — because the part it did say is accurate. The fix above is narrow on purpose: it explicitly tells the judge that an incomplete-but-true summary should be capped at 5. Without that instruction, LLM-as-judge's generosity bias and this task's specific failure mode — partial truths — compound each other into a badly wrong score.

The honest cons
  • It tends to be generous — an LLM judge often rates things "pretty good" even when they're subtly wrong, unless you explicitly ground it against the specific failure mode of your task.
  • It can be fooled by confident-sounding language, the same way a human skimming quickly can be.
  • It costs money per call — though far less than 50 hours of legal review.
When to use it Large test sets where manual review of everything is infeasible, and as a first-pass filter, with human review reserved for whatever it flags.
Quick recap
  • LLM-as-judge scores at scale in minutes, at a real but modest per-call cost.
  • Its default bias is generosity — an ungrounded prompt rates "technically true but incomplete" summaries too highly.
  • An explicit partial-failure cap, written into the judge prompt itself, directly counters that bias for this task's specific failure mode.
Sub-topic 3 of 7

3. Automatic Metrics

What are... automatic metrics? Computational metrics that compare the generated summary to a reference summary, without any LLM call at all.

The one worth understanding in depth here is semantic similarity: it embeds both summaries and compares meaning, forgiving different wording along the way.

semantic_similarity.py 
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

model = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')

def semantic_similarity(summary_a, summary_b):
    emb_a = model.encode(summary_a)
    emb_b = model.encode(summary_b)
    return util.pytorch_cos_sim(emb_a, emb_b).item()  # 0-1

Here's where this metric actively misleads, for this task specifically:

where_it_misleads.txt 
Reference summary: "You agree to arbitration and waive your right
                     to join a class action lawsuit."

Candidate A: "You agree to settle disputes through arbitration
              instead of going to court, and you give up the
              ability to join a class action."
  -> Semantic similarity: 0.91 (genuinely faithful, different wording)

Candidate B: "You agree to arbitration for any disputes that arise."
  -> Semantic similarity: 0.79 (still reasonably high!)
  -> But this candidate OMITS the class-action waiver entirely --
     arguably the more legally significant part of the clause.

A 0.79 looks "pretty good" on a 0–1 scale. But Candidate B is missing the single most consequential detail in the clause. Semantic similarity measures how close in meaning space two pieces of text are — it has no concept of "which omitted detail matters more," because it was never told there's a hierarchy of importance within the clause. This is the central limitation to understand: semantic similarity catches paraphrasing well and catches selective omission poorly, because an incomplete-but-related summary is still semantically close to a complete one.

When to use it As a fast, free sanity check across a large set, paired with a method that can catch omission — never as the only signal for a task where missing details are dangerous.
Quick recap
  • Automatic metrics like semantic similarity are computational, fast, and free — no LLM call required.
  • Semantic similarity catches paraphrasing well but catches selective omission poorly — a 0.79 can hide a missing, legally significant detail.
  • It has no concept of which detail within a passage matters most — it only measures overall closeness in meaning space.
  • Use it as a fast sanity check paired with an omission-aware method, never alone for a task where missing details are dangerous.
Sub-topic 4 of 7

4. Human-in-the-Loop

What is... human-in-the-loop evaluation? Combine automation (covers everything) with humans (covers what automation gets wrong) — LLM-as-judge scores all 1,500, a lawyer reviews only the cases that need it.

The routing decision matters more than the math. For this task, route to human review when:

  • LLM-as-judge score is in the uncertain middle (4–7), not just low scores.
  • Semantic similarity is moderate-high (0.70–0.85) — exactly the band where Candidate B above lives, since a clean 0.95+ is probably genuinely faithful and a clean <0.5 is probably genuinely bad, but the middle band is where omission hides.
  • The clause type is flagged as high-risk (arbitration, class-action waivers, indemnification) regardless of any score, because the cost of missing a real failure here is much higher than for a low-stakes clause like a notice-period definition.
routing.py 
def needs_human_review(llm_score, semantic_sim, clause_type):
    HIGH_RISK_CLAUSES = {"arbitration", "class_action_waiver", "indemnification"}

    if clause_type in HIGH_RISK_CLAUSES:
        return True  # always human-reviewed, regardless of score
    if 4 <= llm_score <= 7:
        return True  # uncertain LLM judgment
    if 0.70 <= semantic_sim <= 0.85:
        return True  # the omission-prone middle band
    return False
Why this routing logic looks like it does
  • It's built around the specific failure mode identified in Part 3 — the omission-prone middle band of semantic similarity — not a generic "flag anything uncertain" rule.
  • A human-in-the-loop system is only as good as the routing logic deciding what reaches the human; if you don't know your method's specific blind spot, you can't route around it.
Go Deeper: why not just route on "low score" and be done with it?

Because for this task, the dangerous failures don't cluster at the bottom of the scale — they cluster in the middle. A summary that's mostly right but silently drops one consequential clause doesn't read as obviously bad to an automated metric; it reads as "pretty close." A naive threshold tuned to catch obviously bad outputs is, by construction, blind to the specific failure this task cares about most. The lesson generalizes past contracts: whatever your system's most expensive failure mode actually looks like in the numbers, your routing rule has to be built to catch that shape, not a generic "review anything that looks uncertain" rule borrowed from a different problem.

Quick recap
  • Human-in-the-loop combines automation's coverage with a human's judgment on exactly the cases automation is least trustworthy on.
  • The routing rule, not the raw math, is what determines whether the system actually works.
  • Route on the middle band of similarity, the uncertain-middle LLM score, and high-risk categories — not just "anything scoring low."

5. Building an Evaluation Harness

What is... an evaluation harness? A harness runs all of this together — rubric grading (on a small subset), LLM-as-judge (on everything), semantic similarity (on everything), and the routing logic — and reports a verdict per prompt variant.
eval_harness.py 
class ContractSummaryEvalHarness:
    def __init__(self, golden_dataset, reference_summaries):
        self.dataset = golden_dataset  # clause texts + clause types
        self.references = reference_summaries  # human-verified summaries
        self.results = {}

    def eval_variant(self, summarize_fn, variant_name):
        scores = {"llm_judge": [], "semantic": [], "needs_review": []}

        for clause in self.dataset:
            summary = summarize_fn(clause["text"])
            reference = self.references[clause["id"]]

            llm_score = llm_score_summary(clause["text"], summary)
            sem_sim = semantic_similarity(summary, reference)
            flagged = needs_human_review(llm_score, sem_sim, clause["type"])

            scores["llm_judge"].append(llm_score)
            scores["semantic"].append(sem_sim)
            scores["needs_review"].append(flagged)

        self.results[variant_name] = {
            "avg_llm_judge": sum(scores["llm_judge"]) / len(scores["llm_judge"]),
            "avg_semantic": sum(scores["semantic"]) / len(scores["semantic"]),
            "pct_flagged_for_review": (sum(scores["needs_review"]) /
                                        len(scores["needs_review"])) * 100,
        }
        return self.results[variant_name]

    def compare(self, variant_names):
        print("\nVariant Comparison:")
        for name in variant_names:
            r = self.results[name]
            print(f"\n{name}:")
            print(f"  LLM-judge avg:        {r['avg_llm_judge']:.1f}/10")
            print(f"  Semantic similarity:  {r['avg_semantic']:.2f}")
            print(f"  % needing human review: {r['pct_flagged_for_review']:.1f}%")

The third metric — % flagged for human review — is arguably the most useful one for a decision-maker, even though it's not a "quality score" at all. A prompt variant with a slightly lower LLM-judge average but a much lower flag rate may be the better choice in practice, because it means less human review time at scale. A harness that only reports quality scores would miss that trade-off entirely.

Quick recap
  • A harness runs rubric grading, LLM-as-judge, semantic similarity, and routing logic together, and reports a verdict per variant.
  • "% flagged for human review" is a decision-relevant number even though it's not itself a quality score — it captures the practical cost of shipping a variant.

6. Combining Methods — A Worked Comparison

Suppose the team tests 3 prompt variants on the same 50-clause golden dataset:

variant_comparison.txt 
Variant A ("concise"): instructed to keep summaries under 15 words
  LLM-judge avg: 6.8/10
  Semantic similarity avg: 0.81
  % flagged for review: 38%
  -> Short summaries frequently omit secondary details (the class-action
     waiver problem from Part 3) to hit the word count

Variant B ("thorough"): instructed to mention every material term
  LLM-judge avg: 8.9/10
  Semantic similarity avg: 0.93
  % flagged for review: 9%
  -> Longer, but catches the details that matter

Variant C ("plain language emphasis"): instructed to prioritize a
  non-lawyer being able to understand it, with no length constraint
  LLM-judge avg: 8.1/10
  Semantic similarity avg: 0.88
  % flagged for review: 14%
  -> Slightly less complete than B in rare cases, but the rubric scores
     from a manual review of its flagged 14% show it's still catching
     the high-risk clause types correctly -- it just phrases things more
     casually, which the LLM-judge mildly penalizes despite no real
     accuracy loss

Reading these three numbers together tells a story none of them tells alone. Variant A's lower semantic similarity and high flag rate point to the same root cause: omission for brevity. Variant B's high scores across the board make it look like the clear winner — but the team still needs the manual rubric on its flagged 9% to confirm those flags are genuinely low-risk and not high-risk clauses slipping through quietly. Variant C looks worse than B by the numbers, but a 10-minute manual rubric check on its flagged cases reveals the LLM-judge's score is penalizing tone, not accuracy — information no single automated number would have surfaced.

The takeaway, stated plainly
  • The highest average score and the best real system aren't automatically the same thing.
  • A small, targeted manual check on the cases an automated method flagged is what turns three numbers into an actual, defensible decision.
Quick recap
  • Three variants' numbers, read together, reveal root causes (omission-for-brevity, genuine thoroughness, tone-vs-accuracy) that no single metric shows alone.
  • Even a "clear winner" on paper still needs a manual spot-check of its flagged cases before you trust the ranking.

7. Common Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Trusting semantic similarity alone for a task where omission is dangerous As shown in Part 3, a 0.79 similarity score can hide a missing class-action waiver. Always pair it with a method that checks completeness.
Pitfall 2: Letting LLM-as-judge's generosity bias go unchecked An ungrounded "is this accurate?" prompt will rate an incomplete-but-true summary too highly. The fix is a judge prompt that explicitly defines what counts as a partial failure (Part 2).
Pitfall 3: Routing to human review based only on "low score" The omission problem hides in the middle band of semantic similarity, not the bottom. A naive "review anything under 0.5" rule misses exactly the failures this task cares most about.
Pitfall 4: Picking the variant with the single highest average score Variant C's lower LLM-judge score, once manually checked, turned out to reflect tone, not accuracy. Without checking the actual flagged cases, the team would have under-rated a perfectly good variant.
Pitfall 5: Never validating the automated methods against human judgment at all If you never compare LLM-judge or semantic similarity scores to actual human review on at least a sample, you have no way of knowing which of these pitfalls is actively happening in your own system.

Thought process: the real skill here isn't asking an assistant to "build an eval harness" — it's specifying the exact failure mode you're defending against. A vague request gets you a harness that computes averages; a precise one gets you a harness that explicitly caps partial-truth scores and routes on the deceptive middle band rather than a naive low-score threshold.

Quick recap
  • Five pitfalls, one root cause: trusting a single number without understanding what it can and can't see.
  • Every fix in this chapter is the same shape — know the specific failure mode, then design the check or routing rule around that shape, not a generic rule borrowed from elsewhere.
In the Real World
  • Teams that ship LLM-judged eval pipelines almost always discover their judge's generosity bias the hard way first, then add an explicit partial-failure or completeness clause after the fact — building it in from the start, as this chapter does, saves that painful discovery cycle.
  • "% flagged for human review" quietly becomes the real operating metric in many production eval setups, even when a "quality score" is what gets reported upward — because it's the number that actually predicts review-team workload.
  • Periodically re-validating an automated method against a fresh batch of real human judgment is the single habit that separates teams who know their blind spots from teams who are flying blind and don't know it.

Points to Remember

  • No single method is reliable in isolation for this kind of faithfulness check — each one has a known blind spot: rubric grading is slow, LLM-as-judge is generous, semantic similarity is blind to omission.
  • Know your task's specific failure mode, then design your routing and grading logic around THAT — not a generic "flag low scores" rule.
  • The most useful output of an eval harness might not be a quality score at all — "% needing human review" can be the decision-relevant number.
  • A worse-looking automated score doesn't always mean a worse system. Check what's actually driving the number before ranking variants by it.
  • Validate your automated methods against human judgment periodically, or you'll never know which blind spot is currently biting you.
