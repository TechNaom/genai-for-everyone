Session 5.4 Interview Prep 10 min read

Interview Questions: Bias Measurement & Responsible AI

Bias and fairness questions are where interviewers separate candidates who can recite "AI can be biased" from candidates who can actually design a measurement, name a concrete trade-off, or explain why a stakeholder's confident claim doesn't hold up. These questions probe exactly that. Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 A stakeholder says, "I tested our hiring tool on a resume from a woman and it gave a fair result — so we're good on bias." What's wrong with this claim?
Strong answer

Bias is a comparative property — a single output tells you nothing about whether treatment is unequal. A real bias check requires matched variants (the same underlying qualifications, varied only in a demographic or framing detail) and comparing the outputs across them. One fair-looking output doesn't rule out disparities that would show up across many comparisons, or in specific edge cases. A strong answer also notes that bias checks should be an explicit, ongoing category of testing — the five-step audit framework from this session, not a one-time spot check.

Red flags

Accepting the stakeholder's claim as sufficient, not mentioning the need for a controlled comparison, or treating bias testing as a single pass/fail check rather than an ongoing discipline.

Follow-up

"Design two matched test cases you'd use to check this hiring tool for bias." (Strong answer: two resumes identical in every qualification, differing only in a name signaling gender or ethnicity, then compare the model's recommendation and score for each.)

2 A model achieves higher overall accuracy by performing very well on the majority group and noticeably worse on a minority group. Is this an acceptable trade-off?
Strong answer

This is a fairness/parity concern, not just an aggregate accuracy question — optimizing for overall accuracy can mask real harm concentrated in a subgroup. Acceptability depends on stakes: a hiring, lending, or healthcare decision has a much lower tolerance for this gap than a low-stakes recommendation feature. Concrete responses include reporting per-group metrics separately (never just the aggregate), setting explicit parity thresholds, adjusting decision thresholds per group, or accepting a small aggregate accuracy cost in exchange for better parity.

Red flags

"Higher overall accuracy is always better" with no acknowledgment of distributional harm, no mention of stakes or context mattering, or no concrete mitigation proposed at all.

Follow-up

"How would you explain this trade-off to a stakeholder who only cares about the headline accuracy number?" (Strong answer: show the per-group breakdown next to the aggregate number, and frame the gap in terms of concrete harm to real people, not just a second statistic.)

3 Walk me through the difference between demographic parity, equal opportunity, and calibration — and give an example where satisfying one would make another one worse.
Strong answer

Demographic parity asks whether outcome rates match across groups regardless of underlying qualification rates; equal opportunity asks whether people who genuinely deserve a positive outcome get it at the same rate across groups; calibration asks whether a stated confidence level (e.g. "80% likely") means the same thing across every group. A strong candidate can produce a concrete example where they conflict: if the true qualification rate genuinely differs between two groups, forcing demographic parity (equal approval rates) will push calibration out of alignment, since a well-calibrated model would naturally produce different approval rates when the underlying rates differ. This is exactly why "no single definition of fairness" isn't a hedge — it's a mathematical reality.

Red flags

Treating the three metrics as interchangeable, being unable to produce a concrete conflicting example, or claiming one metric is simply "the correct" definition of fairness in all cases.

Follow-up

"Which of the three would you prioritize for a medical screening tool, and why?" (Strong answer: likely equal opportunity, since missing a real case across any group is the costliest failure mode — but a strong candidate reasons through it rather than reciting a rule.)

4 Amazon's hiring bot and the COMPAS recidivism tool were both biased, without anyone intentionally designing them to discriminate. How does that happen, and what does it imply about how you'd audit a new system?
Strong answer

Both systems learned patterns already present in historical data — a decade of male-dominated tech hiring, or historical bias already baked into the criminal justice system — and reproduced those patterns faithfully, exactly as a model is supposed to do with its training data. The implication for auditing a new system: you can't assume good intentions or a clean-looking spec means a clean outcome. You have to actually measure outcomes across groups on real (or representative) data, because the bias lives in the data's history, not in anyone's stated intent.

Red flags

Assuming bias requires malicious intent, or concluding "so bias is unavoidable, nothing to be done" instead of naming concrete mitigations and audit steps.

Follow-up

"The company removes the biased tool's protected-attribute fields entirely. Is that sufficient?" (Strong answer: no — per the healthcare AI incident, a proxy variable correlated with the protected attribute can reproduce the same bias even when the attribute itself is never an input.)

5 You're asked to write a model card for a system with a real accuracy gap between two groups. What goes in it, and what would you say in the "recommended use" section?
Strong answer

A model card documents overall accuracy, accuracy broken out per group (not hidden behind the aggregate), known limitations stated plainly (e.g. "lower accuracy for group B"), and a recommended-use statement grounded in the actual findings — e.g. "not recommended for high-stakes decisions without human review" when a real disparity was found, versus "acceptable for medium-stakes decisions, monitor continuously" when parity checks pass. A strong candidate treats the model card as an honesty tool, not a marketing document — its entire value comes from stating the real limitations, not from making the system look good.

Red flags

Suggesting the model card only report the overall accuracy number, treating "recommended use" as boilerplate rather than tied to the actual findings, or omitting known limitations to make the write-up look cleaner.

Follow-up

"A PM asks you to soften the 'not recommended for high-stakes use' language before a launch review. What do you do?" (Strong answer: push back — a model card exists specifically to give decision-makers accurate information; softening a real, measured limitation defeats its entire purpose.)
