Session 5.4 Intermediate 40–45 min read

Responsible AI & Bias in Practice

Your model works well. Technically sound. Passes safety tests. And then a hiring chatbot recommends men 40% more often than women, or a loan approval system denies loans to minorities at higher rates. These aren't bugs — they're bias: systematic unfairness baked into the model. This chapter teaches you to recognize it, measure it, and reduce it.

On this page 1. Types of Bias 2. Measuring Bias 3. Audit Framework 4. Mitigations 5. Real Incidents 6. Fairness Framework 7. Accessibility
A customer support bot responds better to some accents than others. An image captioner misses people with disabilities. A hiring chatbot recommends men 40% more often than women. None of these systems were built by anyone trying to discriminate — each one shipped after looking technically fine, maybe even after passing a safety review from Session 5.3. That's exactly what makes bias different from most bugs you've dealt with so far: it doesn't throw an error, it doesn't crash, and it can sail straight through testing that only ever checked "does it work," never "does it work the same for everyone." This chapter is about closing that gap — naming the specific shapes bias takes, measuring it with real numbers instead of a gut feeling, running a structured audit, and knowing what to do once you find something.
Sub-topic 1 of 7

1. Types of Bias

"Bias" isn't one thing. It shows up in at least four distinct shapes, each with its own cause, its own concrete impact, and its own mitigation — which matters, because the fix for one type usually doesn't touch the others at all.

What is... representation bias? Some groups are under-represented in the training data itself. If training data has 10% women in technical roles, the model learns "engineers are mostly male" — not because anyone told it that, but because that's what the data looked like. Impact: career advice biased against women, hiring systems that favor male candidates, women feeling excluded from the system's outputs. Mitigation: audit training data composition, oversample underrepresented groups, use balanced datasets.
What is... performance bias? The model performs differently across groups. A speech-to-text system with a 35% error rate for Latinx accents versus 5% for standard US English is performance bias in action. Impact: deaf and hard-of-hearing users with certain accents can't use the system at all — an accessibility barrier and a form of exclusion by the technology itself. Mitigation: benchmark performance per group (not just in aggregate), collect data from diverse speakers, and treat "improve fairness" as a real, trackable model-quality goal, not an afterthought.
What is... allocation bias? The system denies resources or opportunities unfairly. A loan approval system that denies 30% of applications from Black applicants versus 10% from white applicants is allocation bias. Impact: financial discrimination, perpetuated inequality, and in many jurisdictions, this is flatly illegal, not just an ethical gray area. Mitigation: monitor approval rates by group on an ongoing basis, audit historical data the system was trained or tuned on for pre-existing bias, and adjust thresholds deliberately for fairness.
What is... associational bias? The model learns harmful associations baked into language itself — "doctor" implicitly maps to male, "nurse" to female, "CEO" to white. Impact: reinforces stereotypes, users internalize biased beliefs from a system they trust, and the bias perpetuates societal inequality rather than correcting for it. Mitigation: audit word associations directly, debias embeddings, and prefer gender-neutral language in prompts and outputs where it doesn't sacrifice clarity.
four_types_at_a_glance.txt 
Representation bias  -- who's in the training data at all?
Performance bias      -- does the model work equally well for everyone?
Allocation bias       -- who gets the resource/opportunity/approval?
Associational bias    -- what stereotypes does the model's language reinforce?

Each has a different root cause -- so a single "debias the model" effort
rarely fixes more than one of these at a time.
Quick recap
  • Representation bias: some groups under-represented in training data.
  • Performance bias: the model works less well for some groups than others.
  • Allocation bias: resources or opportunities denied unfairly across groups.
  • Associational bias: harmful stereotypes learned and reinforced through language.
  • None of these are intentional — they're systematic, coming from data and design choices, not malice.

2. Measuring Bias

Naming the types of bias only gets you so far — you need actual numbers to know whether a system has a problem, and how big it is. Four metrics cover most of what shows up in practice.

Metric 1: Demographic Parity Do groups get equal treatment, full stop — regardless of any underlying difference in the group's actual qualification rate?
demographic_parity.txt 
Approval rate for group A = 80%
Approval rate for group B = 50%

Difference = 30% (bias exists)
Target: <5% difference
Metric 2: Equal Opportunity Among people who genuinely deserve a positive outcome, do all groups have an equal chance of actually receiving it?
equal_opportunity.txt 
True positive rate for group A = 90%
True positive rate for group B = 70%

Difference = 20% (bias exists)
Metric 3: Calibration When the model expresses a confidence level, does that confidence mean the same thing across every group?
calibration.txt 
When model predicts 80% likelihood:
- Group A: 80% actually approve -> Calibrated
- Group B: 60% actually approve -> Biased
Metric 4: Representation Do groups appear fairly across the model's actual outputs — not just in the training data, but in what it recommends day to day?
representation_metric.txt 
Occupations in job recommendations:
- Female candidates: 40% tech, 60% non-tech
- Male candidates: 60% tech, 40% non-tech

Difference suggests bias

Try it yourself: the calculation behind all four metrics is the same basic move — take a rate for each group, then measure the gap between the largest and smallest. Run the playground below with the demographic-parity numbers above, then edit the rates to see how the gap (and the pass/fail call against a 5% target) changes.

parity_gap_demo.py
Click "Run Code" — then edit the rates dict and re-run.
Quick recap
  • Demographic parity: equal outcome rates across groups.
  • Equal opportunity: equal true positive rates among those who deserve a positive outcome.
  • Calibration: a stated confidence level means the same thing for every group.
  • Representation: groups appear fairly across actual outputs, not just training data.
  • All four reduce to the same basic move: compute a rate per group, then measure the gap.

3. Audit Framework

Measuring one metric once is a spot check. A real bias audit is a repeatable, five-step process — the same shape the Core Path exercise for this session walks you through end to end.

Step 1: Define groups Decide, up front, which protected attributes the audit will actually compare across.
step1_define_groups.txt 
Protected attributes:
- Gender (male, female, non-binary)
- Race/ethnicity (self-identified)
- Age (18-30, 31-50, 51+)
- Disability status (yes/no)
- Geographic region
Step 2: Collect sample data Gather a large enough, genuinely diverse sample — not a handful of convenient examples you happened to have lying around.
step2_collect_sample.txt 
Sample 1000 model outputs across:
- 5 groups x 5 metrics = 25 conditions
- 40 samples per condition
- Diverse scenarios (not cherry-picked)
Step 3: Annotate ground truth Human annotators label each output — this is what makes the difference between an accuracy number and a guess.
step3_annotate.txt 
For each output, human annotators label:
- Is it accurate? (Yes/No)
- Is it helpful? (Yes/No/Partial)
- Is it fair? (Yes/No)
- Does it respect dignity? (Yes/No)
Step 4: Calculate metrics per group Apply the metrics from Part 2 to every group separately, then look at the gaps.
step4_calculate_metrics.txt 
For group A:
- Accuracy: 92%
- Helpfulness: 85%
- Fairness: 88%

For group B:
- Accuracy: 75%
- Helpfulness: 60%
- Fairness: 50%

Gaps suggest bias
Step 5: Document findings Write the findings down formally, with a severity rating — a bias audit that lives only in someone's head doesn't help the next person who touches the system.
step5_bias_audit_report.txt 
BIAS AUDIT REPORT
==================

Finding 1: Accuracy disparity
- Group A: 92% accurate
- Group B: 75% accurate
- Gap: 17%
- Severity: HIGH

Finding 2: Representation
- Group A appears 60% of outputs
- Group B appears 40% of outputs
- Gap: 20%
- Severity: MEDIUM

Before you open this session's starter file, it's worth writing the prompt you'd hand an AI assistant to help scaffold an audit script like this — the design decisions (what counts as a group, what "accurate" means for this dataset, what severity threshold triggers a finding) are the part that actually matters, not the boilerplate loop that computes a percentage.

Thought process: the real skill isn't asking an assistant to "check my model for bias" — it's specifying the audit's scope and criteria up front: which groups, which metrics, what threshold counts as a finding, and what a finding's severity should be based on. A vague request gets you a script that prints some percentages with no way to tell whether any of them actually matter.

Write the prompt you'd give an AI assistant to build this:

Paste your prompt into Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, or whatever assistant you use, and compare what it builds against this session's exercise — does it let you name the groups and metrics explicitly, and does every finding come with a severity level tied to an actual threshold? That comparison is the real test, not a score.
Show a strong version of this prompt 
Write a Python class called BiasAudit that takes a list of model output dicts (each with a "group", an "occupation" or category, and a "score"). Implement methods to calculate: (1) accuracy per group, using a score threshold I can configure, (2) representation per group as a percentage of the total, (3) occupational/category distribution per group. Then write a generate_report() method that prints all three sections, computes the gap between the highest and lowest group for accuracy and representation, and prints an explicit FINDING line with a severity label when a gap exceeds a configurable target (default 5% for accuracy, 10% for representation). End the report with a short, concrete list of mitigation suggestions tied to whatever findings were actually triggered, not a generic list.
Quick recap
  • A real audit is five steps: define groups, collect a diverse sample, annotate ground truth with humans, calculate metrics per group, and document findings with severity.
  • Documentation matters as much as the numbers — an undocumented finding doesn't help the next person who touches the system.
  • The valuable design decisions are scope and criteria (which groups, which metrics, what threshold), not the loop that computes a percentage.

4. Mitigations

Finding bias is only half the job. Once an audit surfaces a gap, four concrete levers exist to actually reduce it — each intervening at a different point in the system's lifecycle.

Mitigation 1: Data Balancing

Oversample underrepresented groups so training data stops skewing the model's assumptions.

data_balancing.py 
# Oversample underrepresented groups
from sklearn.utils import resample

df_majority = data[data['group'] == 'A']
df_minority = data[data['group'] == 'B']

# Oversample minority
df_minority_upsampled = resample(df_minority,
                                 n_samples=len(df_majority),
                                 random_state=1)

df_balanced = pd.concat([df_majority, df_minority_upsampled])

Mitigation 2: Fairness Constraints

Add a fairness objective directly into training, alongside the model's normal loss function.

fairness_constraint.py 
# During training, add fairness objective
fairness_loss = abs(accuracy_group_A - accuracy_group_B)
total_loss = model_loss + lambda_weight * fairness_loss

Mitigation 3: Post-Hoc Adjustment

After training is done, adjust decision thresholds per group rather than retraining the model from scratch.

post_hoc_adjustment.py 
# After model training, adjust thresholds
# Use different thresholds per group

if group == 'A':
    threshold = 0.5
elif group == 'B':
    threshold = 0.3  # Lower threshold for underserved group

prediction = 1 if score > threshold else 0

Mitigation 4: Transparency & Documentation

Document exactly what the model does and doesn't do well, in a model card — the Pro Path exercise builds exactly this.

model_card.txt 
Model Card (document model behavior):
- Overall accuracy: 85%
- Accuracy per group: A=92%, B=75%
- Known limitations: Lower accuracy for group B
- Recommended use: Not recommended for high-stakes decisions
  without human review.
Choosing among the four
  • Data balancing and fairness constraints require access to the training pipeline — they're not available if you're only consuming someone else's model through an API.
  • Post-hoc adjustment and transparency/documentation work regardless of who trained the model — which is why they're usually the first two levers available to most teams building on top of a foundation model.
  • None of the four is "the" fix. A serious response to a real finding usually combines more than one.
Quick recap
  • Data balancing: oversample underrepresented groups before/during training.
  • Fairness constraints: add a fairness term directly to the training objective.
  • Post-hoc adjustment: use different decision thresholds per group after training.
  • Transparency & documentation: a model card that states accuracy per group, limitations, and recommended use.
  • Data-level mitigations require training access; threshold and documentation mitigations don't — a useful split when you're building on someone else's model.

5. Real Incidents

None of this is hypothetical. Four well-documented, real-world incidents show every type of bias from Part 1 causing genuine harm at scale.

Incident 1: Amazon Hiring Bot
  • What happened: Amazon's ML hiring tool showed bias against women.
  • Why: training data came from 10 years of male-dominated tech hiring.
  • Impact: women were less likely to be recommended for roles.
  • Lesson: bias in training data becomes bias in the model — the model didn't invent the pattern, it learned it.
Incident 2: COMPAS Recidivism
  • What happened: a criminal risk-assessment tool was biased against Black defendants.
  • Why: historical bias already present in the criminal justice system.
  • Impact: higher false-positive rates for Black defendants — flagged as high-risk more often, even when they weren't.
  • Lesson: historical bias in the data a system learns from becomes perpetuated bias in its decisions.
Incident 3: Google Images
  • What happened: image search for "CEO" showed mostly white men.
  • Why: web images already reflected societal bias, compounded by search-ranking bias.
  • Impact: reinforced the stereotype that CEOs are white men, to anyone who searched.
  • Lesson: representation matters, and defaults matter — what a system shows by default shapes what users come to expect as normal.
Incident 4: Healthcare AI
  • What happened: a model for patient health-risk scoring used race as a proxy for socioeconomic status.
  • Why: race happened to correlate with healthcare access, itself a product of existing inequality.
  • Impact: Black patients were assessed as lower-risk and denied care they actually needed.
  • Lesson: proxies for sensitive attributes can cause real harm even when the sensitive attribute itself is never used directly.
Go Deeper: why does "we didn't use race as an input" not settle the healthcare case?

Because a model doesn't need a field literally labeled "race" to end up making race-correlated decisions — it just needs some other input (healthcare spending, in the actual case) that happens to correlate with race for reasons rooted in historical inequality. This is exactly why an audit has to look at outcomes across groups, not just check whether a sensitive attribute appears in the model's input schema. Removing the field doesn't remove the correlation the model can still find through everything else that's left.

Quick recap
  • Amazon's hiring bot and COMPAS both show historical bias in training data becoming bias in the model's decisions.
  • Google Images shows how representation and defaults reinforce stereotypes at scale.
  • Healthcare AI shows that a proxy variable can reproduce bias even when the sensitive attribute itself is never used as an input.

6. Fairness Framework

There is no single, universal definition of "fair." Which definition applies depends on context, and a serious system has to choose one deliberately rather than assuming there's one obvious right answer.

fairness_definitions.txt 
Fairness Type          Definition                              Example
---------------------- ---------------------------------------- ------------------------------------------
Demographic Parity     Equal treatment across groups             Same % approval for all groups
Equalized Odds         Equal true & false positive rates         Same hiring accuracy for all genders
Calibration            Predictions equally accurate per group    Model 80% confident = 80% likely, for all
Individual Fairness    Similar individuals treated similarly     Two similar applicants get similar scores
The trade-off you can't avoid You can't optimize for all of these simultaneously — it's a mathematical fact, not a limitation of current tools. Satisfying demographic parity exactly can force calibration to break, and vice versa, when the underlying base rates genuinely differ between groups. The practical response: choose which definition matters most based on stakeholder values and the stakes of the decision, and be transparent about which one you chose and why.
Go Deeper: how do you actually choose, for a specific system?

Ask what a false negative and a false positive each cost in this specific context. For a loan approval system, demographic parity in approval rates might matter most to regulators and to public trust. For a medical screening tool, equalized odds (catching real cases at the same rate across groups) might matter more than raw approval-rate parity, since missing a real case is far more costly than a false alarm. There's no formula that picks the answer for you — but naming the cost of each error type, for each group, is what turns "it depends" into an actual decision you can defend and document.

Quick recap
  • Four common fairness definitions: demographic parity, equalized odds, calibration, individual fairness.
  • You cannot optimize for all of them at once — it's a real trade-off, not a tooling gap.
  • Choose a definition based on context and stakeholder values, and document the choice transparently.

7. Accessibility

Fairness isn't only about the protected-attribute groups from Part 3. It's about everyone — and accessibility is where that broader idea becomes concrete and checkable.

Accessibility dimensions Vision: blind users, low vision, color blindness. Hearing: deaf users, hard of hearing. Motor: users who can't use a keyboard or mouse. Cognitive: dyslexia, ADHD, autism. Language: non-native speakers, different dialects.
accessibility_audit_checklist.txt 
[ ] Does the system work with screen readers?
[ ] Are captions/transcripts provided?
[ ] Can users interact without a mouse?
[ ] Is text large enough (18pt+)?
[ ] Are color contrasts sufficient (4.5:1)?
[ ] Is language clear and simple?
[ ] Are there multiple ways to complete tasks?
Why accessibility belongs in a bias chapter
  • Performance bias (Part 1) and accessibility are the same underlying problem, viewed from two angles: does the system work equally well for everyone who might use it.
  • Accessibility fixes tend to help far beyond their original target — captions help everyone in a noisy room, clear simple language helps every non-expert user, not just non-native speakers.
  • An audit (Part 3) that never checks accessibility has a systematic blind spot, even if every demographic-parity number looks clean.
Quick recap
  • Accessibility spans vision, hearing, motor, cognitive, and language dimensions — not just "protected attribute" groups.
  • A concrete, checkable audit checklist exists: screen readers, captions, keyboard-only interaction, text size, color contrast, plain language, multiple task paths.
  • Accessibility fixes tend to benefit everyone, not only the group they were designed for.
In the Real World
  • Every real incident in Part 5 shipped after looking technically fine — bias audits catch a category of problem that ordinary functional testing structurally cannot see.
  • Model cards (Mitigation 4) are becoming a real industry norm, not a nice-to-have — expect to be asked for one whenever a model touches a hiring, lending, or healthcare decision.
  • Session 5.5 picks up directly from here: once you've measured bias and other safety gaps, guardrails are how you implement the actual defenses and mitigations in a running system.

Points to Remember

  • Bias is systematic, not intentional. It comes from data and design choices, not malicious intent.
  • You can't ignore bias. It causes real, documented harm — hiring, lending, healthcare, criminal justice.
  • Measure fairness explicitly. What you measure, you improve; what you never check, you never catch.
  • Different fairness definitions exist, and you can't satisfy all of them at once — choose based on context, and be transparent about the choice.
  • Representation matters. Track which groups actually appear in a system's outputs, not just its training data.
  • Accessibility benefits everyone. Clear captions, plain language, and keyboard navigation help far beyond their original target group.
  • There's no perfect fairness. Trade-offs are unavoidable — the responsible move is being transparent about which ones you made.
