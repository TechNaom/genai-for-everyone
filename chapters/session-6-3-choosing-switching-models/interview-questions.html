Session 6.3 Interview Prep 10 min read

Interview Questions: Choosing & Switching Models

Model-selection and reliability questions come up constantly once a role involves shipping GenAI features past the prototype stage — interviewers use them to check whether you think about production trade-offs, not just whether a demo works. Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 When would you recommend self-hosting an open-weight model instead of using a closed API, given that the closed API is probably higher quality?
Strong answer

Data residency/compliance requirements that prohibit sending data to a third party; extremely high, steady-state volume where fixed infrastructure cost undercuts per-token pricing; and an acknowledgment of the trade-off — self-hosting adds real operational burden (uptime, scaling, staying current with new releases) most projects don't need on day one. A strong answer also notes that most teams should default to a closed API and move only when a specific driver appears.

Red flags

"Self-hosting is always cheaper" (ignores infrastructure and operational cost); no mention of compliance/data-residency as a real driver; treating this as a purely technical decision with no business context.

Follow-up

"Your self-hosted model needs to be updated to a newer open-weight release. What does that process look like compared to a closed API's automatic updates?"

2 Your codebase calls a specific provider's SDK directly in 40 different files. What's the risk, and how would you fix it?
Strong answer

Risk: switching providers, adding a fallback, or upgrading the SDK requires finding and changing all 40 call sites — slow and error-prone, with a real chance of missing one. Fix: introduce an adapter layer — a single function or module wrapping provider calls — so business logic calls one consistent interface regardless of provider. A strong answer also mentions that this is what makes adding a fallback strategy cheap later.

Red flags

Not identifying the maintainability risk clearly; proposing "just rewrite all 40 files" as the fix rather than introducing an abstraction; no mention of how this affects fallback design.

Follow-up

"Design the function signature for that adapter layer."

3 You've built a fallback: if the primary provider times out, retry against a secondary model. In production, the primary is experiencing intermittent slowness (not full outages), and 15% of requests are triggering the fallback unnecessarily. What's wrong, and how would you fix it?
Strong answer

Diagnoses that the fallback's trigger condition (a raw timeout) is too sensitive to transient slowness, not just genuine failures. Proposes a fix: a more tolerant timeout, or a circuit-breaker pattern that only engages the fallback after sustained failures rather than per-request. Recognizes the trade-off: falling back too eagerly serves users a weaker model unnecessarily often.

Red flags

"The fallback is working as designed, no issue" (misses the real problem); no concrete fix proposed; failing to distinguish transient slowness from genuine outages.

Follow-up

"How would a circuit breaker specifically prevent this compared to a per-request timeout retry?"

4 True or false: most teams should start with a self-hosted open model to avoid vendor lock-in from day one. Defend your answer.
Strong answer

False. Most teams should start with a closed API because setup cost is near zero, and move to self-hosting only when a specific driver (data residency, extreme steady volume, a needed guarantee) appears. "Vendor lock-in" is solved by an adapter layer, not by self-hosting from day one — self-hosting up front adds real infrastructure burden most projects don't need yet, and doesn't even fully avoid switching costs on its own.

Red flags

Answering "true" without qualification; conflating "avoiding vendor lock-in" with "avoiding closed APIs entirely" instead of pointing to the adapter pattern as the actual fix.

Follow-up

"If not self-hosting, what actually protects a team from vendor lock-in on day one?"

5 What is a circuit breaker pattern, and why is it better than retrying a failing provider on every single request?
Strong answer

A circuit breaker stops sending requests to a provider after N consecutive failures, waiting out a cooldown period before trying again — instead of retrying (and adding latency and cost) on every single request during an outage. It's better because repeatedly hitting a provider that's clearly down wastes time and resources on requests very likely to fail anyway, and adds needless latency to the user-facing request path.

Red flags

Describing a circuit breaker as just "a fallback" without the cooldown/consecutive-failure mechanism; not explaining the cost of retrying a doomed request on every call.

Follow-up

"How would you decide the right value for N (the failure count) and the cooldown duration?"

Strategy Tips

  • Cover the "Strong answer" line, answer out loud, then check yourself against it — that's how these are meant to be used for self-testing.
  • Interviewers are listening for trade-off reasoning grounded in production reality, not a memorized definition of "adapter pattern." Anchor every answer to a concrete cost: engineering time, latency, or risk.
  • It's fine to think out loud ("if it's a compliance requirement, self-hosting might be the only option regardless of quality...") — the reasoning is what's being evaluated, not just the final word.
