Session 7.1 Interview Prep 10 min read

Interview Questions: Scoping & Proposals

These questions focus on this session's core skill — scoping an ambiguous request into a proposal you could actually defend — pulled from the broader Week 7 interview pool. Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 Your manager says, "Build us an AI feature for the customer portal," with no further detail. What's your first move?
Strong answer

Doesn't start building — scopes the request first. Asks clarifying questions targeting: specific user/problem, an end-to-end description, why AI (and which kind) is actually needed, and how success will be measured. Mentions writing this down as a short proposal before committing engineering time, not just thinking it through silently.

Red flags

Jumps straight to proposing an architecture or technology stack; doesn't ask any clarifying questions; treats "AI feature" as self-evidently well-defined.

Follow-up

"The manager pushes back: 'Just build something, we don't have time for a proposal.' How do you respond?" (Tests whether they can defend the two-minute cost of scoping against the much larger cost of building the wrong thing.)

2 A VP says: "Let's put AI on customer support, it's a mess right now." List at least three meaningfully different projects this could mean, and the one question you'd ask first.
Strong answer

Names at least 3 distinct possible projects (e.g., drafting reply suggestions, auto-routing tickets, predicting escalation risk, summarizing long threads) with different data/eval requirements. Correctly classifies at least one as predictive vs. generative. The clarifying question surfaces which specific pain point (speed, accuracy, agent workload) the VP actually means — the same discipline as answering "what specific problem, for what specific user?" before anything else.

Red flags

Only identifies one possible project, or treats "AI for support" as a single well-defined task; clarifying question is generic ("what do you want?") rather than targeted at surfacing the ambiguity.

Follow-up

"The VP says 'all of the above, whatever's fastest.' How do you push back on scoping this into one project first?"

3 Walk me through a GenAI project you built.
Strong answer

Leads with the problem and specific user, not a list of technologies. Explains the approach and why that approach was chosen over alternatives. Names one specific hard trade-off or decision made during building. States actual success criteria and whether they were met, with real numbers — exactly the shape of a good capstone one-pager, walked through out loud instead of read off the page.

Red flags

"I used LangChain, a vector database, and Claude" with no problem statement or reasoning; no mention of how they know it worked; can't name a single hard decision or trade-off.

Follow-up

"What would you do differently if you rebuilt this from scratch today?"

4 Design a GenAI feature that helps new employees find answers in company documentation. Walk me through your approach.
Strong answer

Starts with clarifying questions (who exactly, what documentation, how is this failing today) before describing architecture — the same scoping-first instinct as the capstone one-pager. Correctly identifies this as a RAG use case and explains why. Addresses an eval plan: how would you know if answers are actually correct and helpful. Mentions at least one operational concern: cost, latency, or what happens when the docs change.

Red flags

Jumps directly into describing a specific tech stack with no scoping questions; no eval plan mentioned at all; doesn't consider what happens as underlying documents change over time.

Follow-up

"How would you know if this system is getting worse over time, without anyone filing a complaint?"

5 You have a week to build a working demo of a GenAI feature. Walk me through how you'd allocate your time.
Strong answer

Builds the thinnest possible end-to-end pipeline first, however crude, before polishing any individual piece. Treats early milestones as go/no-go checkpoints — if behind schedule, simplifies immediately rather than pushing forward on the original plan. Reserves real time for an actual evaluation pass, not just "does it run." This is the same "size it to the time you have" instinct behind picking a finishable capstone scope over an ambitious unfinished one.

Red flags

Plans to perfect one component fully before ever testing the pipeline end to end; no mention of evaluation time in the schedule; no contingency plan if behind schedule partway through.

Follow-up

"Day 4 of 5, you're behind schedule and only have a rough end-to-end pipeline with no eval done yet. What do you cut?"

Strategy Tips

  • Interviewers will often hand you a live ambiguous request ("build us an AI feature") rather than ask you to define scoping in the abstract — practice asking targeted clarifying questions before proposing anything.
  • When walking through your own capstone, lead with the problem and user, not the tech stack — and always have a real number ready for "how do you know it worked."
  • If pushed to skip scoping ("just build something"), have a short, confident answer ready for why a day spent scoping saves far more time than it costs.
  • Pair these with Weeks 1–6's questions as you move deeper into Week 7's case-study and build sessions.
