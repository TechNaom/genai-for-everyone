Session 7.2 Interview Prep 10 min read

Interview Questions: Reading Someone Else's System

Case study day exercises a distinct interview skill: reading an unfamiliar system description and immediately probing its eval, fallback, and drift story — instead of just saying "looks good." These questions pull from the Week 7 pool, chosen for how directly they connect to today's support-ticket-triage case study. Each includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 I'm going to describe a system to you that you've never seen before: it's a support-ticket triage tool that classifies tickets and drafts suggested responses, gated by a confidence score. What would you want to know before you trusted this system?
Strong answer

Ask where evaluation happens and against what dataset. Ask what the fallback/review path is for low-confidence or uncertain cases. Ask how drift would be detected — is the confidence calibration re-validated over time, or was it only checked once at launch. Ask whether guardrails/review requirements are applied consistently across all risk levels, or unevenly — exactly the gap that let the case study's data-loss incident happen.

Red flags

"Looks good to me" with no probing questions; only asking about the model/technology, not evaluation or failure handling; never asking what happens when the system is wrong.

Follow-up

"Given what you now know about this exact system, what's the single most likely place for a hidden failure to be hiding?" (Points toward the retrieval index's document freshness and the inconsistently-applied review gate.)

2 An eval report shows accuracy dropped from 90% to 60% over the past month, with no code changes. Walk me through how you'd diagnose this.
Strong answer

Don't guess at a fix immediately — investigate the specific failing examples first to find the pattern. Consider drift explicitly: has the input distribution changed, has an underlying model or document set changed. Check whether the golden dataset itself is still representative, or needs updating. This is precisely what happened to the ticket classifier: the model never changed, but new product lines shifted the input distribution until confidence scores no longer meant what they used to.

Red flags

Proposing a fix ("just retrain it" or "switch models") before investigating the actual failure pattern; not mentioning drift as a possible cause; treating this as necessarily a code bug.

Follow-up

"You find the drop is concentrated entirely in one specific ticket category. What does that tell you, and what would you check next?" (Points toward a new, unrecognized input type rather than a general model failure.)

3 A stakeholder asks: "Why do we need a human to review every flagged output if the model is usually right?" How do you answer?
Strong answer

Reframe "usually right" as not the relevant bar — the value of human review is specifically in catching the rare, confidently wrong cases. Explain that in high-stakes domains, the cost of a rare miss can vastly outweigh the cost of routine review time. Point to a concrete example: the case study's classifier was well-calibrated on average, but the RAG layer confidently drafted a data-loss-causing response for exactly the kind of rare, high-stakes case a review gate exists to catch.

Red flags

"If it's usually right, we probably don't need review" (misses the entire point of human-in-the-loop design); no concrete example of what a confident-but-wrong failure looks like; not distinguishing stakes/domain as a factor.

Follow-up

"Under what conditions would you feel comfortable removing the human review step for a subset of cases?" (Tests whether they'd ever remove review from a defined high-risk-topic list, versus only from genuinely low-stakes categories.)

4 Design a GenAI feature that helps new employees find answers in company documentation. Walk me through your approach.
Strong answer

Start with clarifying questions before describing architecture. Correctly identify this as a RAG use case and explain why (grounding in real, current documents, not general knowledge). Address an eval plan: how would you know if answers are actually correct and helpful. Mention at least one operational concern — cost, latency, or what happens when the docs change, which is exactly the stale-document gap that caused the case study's incident.

Red flags

Jumping directly into a tech stack with no scoping questions; no eval plan mentioned; not considering what happens as underlying documents change over time.

Follow-up

"How would you know if this system is getting worse over time, without anyone filing a complaint?" (Tests for a monitoring/drift-detection instinct, not just a launch-time eval.)

5 A VP says: "Let's put AI on customer support, it's a mess right now." List at least three meaningfully different projects this could mean, and the one question you'd ask first.
Strong answer

Name at least 3 distinct possible projects — e.g., drafting reply suggestions, auto-routing tickets (the case study's classifier), predicting escalation risk, or summarizing long threads — with different data/eval requirements. Correctly classify at least one as predictive vs. generative, the same distinction that splits the case study's own classifier from its response-drafter. The clarifying question should surface which specific pain point (speed, accuracy, agent workload) the VP actually means.

Red flags

Naming only one possible project, or treating "AI for support" as a single well-defined task; a generic clarifying question ("what do you want?") instead of one targeted at the ambiguity.

Follow-up

"The VP says 'all of the above, whatever's fastest.' How do you push back on scoping this into one project first?"

Strategy Tips

  • Interviewers will often hand you a live, unfamiliar system description rather than ask you to define terms — practice probing eval, fallback, and drift immediately rather than saying "looks good."
  • Postmortem questions reward naming all compounding root causes, not just the first, most obvious one — the case study's incident needed two separate gaps to actually reach a customer.
  • When a stakeholder pushes back on a review gate with "it's usually right," the strong response reframes the question around the cost of rare, confident failures, not average accuracy.
  • Pair these with earlier weeks' questions for a realistic mixed-topic mock interview as you move toward Week 7's mock interview session.
