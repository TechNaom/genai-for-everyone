Interview Questions: Build Discipline Under a Deadline

Interviewers ask about build discipline in the exact shape this session practiced — not "define MVP" but "you have a week, walk me through how you'd spend it," and they listen for whether you actually treat early milestones as decision points or just push forward hoping time works out. Each question below includes what a strong answer sounds like, common red flags, and a natural follow-up. Click any question to expand it.

1 You have a week to build a working demo of a GenAI feature. Walk me through how you'd allocate your time.
Strong answer

Build the thinnest possible end-to-end pipeline first, however crude, before polishing any individual piece. Treat early milestones as go/no-go checkpoints — if behind schedule, simplify immediately rather than pushing forward on the original plan. Reserve real time for an actual evaluation pass, not just "does it run." And mention getting unblocked quickly (asking for help) rather than silently struggling on a single issue for too long.

Red flags

Planning to perfect one component (e.g., retrieval quality) fully before ever testing the pipeline end to end; no mention of evaluation time in the schedule; no contingency plan if behind schedule partway through.

Follow-up

"Day 4 of 5, you're behind schedule and only have a rough end-to-end pipeline with no eval done yet. What do you cut?" (Tests whether the checkpoint discipline is real or just a talking point — a strong answer cuts scope, not the eval pass.)

2 Your manager says, "Build us an AI feature for the customer portal," with no further detail. What's your first move?
Strong answer

Don't start building — scope the request first. Ask clarifying questions targeting the specific user and problem, an end-to-end description of what it does, why AI (and which kind) is actually needed, and how success will be measured. Mention writing this down as a short proposal before committing engineering time. This is the same discipline that makes MVP-first build days work: you can't build the thinnest useful pipeline until you know what "useful" means for this specific request.

Red flags

Jumping straight to proposing an architecture or technology stack; asking no clarifying questions at all; treating "AI feature" as self-evidently well-defined.

Follow-up

"The manager pushes back: 'Just build something, we don't have time for a proposal.' How do you respond?" (Tests whether they can defend scoping discipline under real pushback, not just describe it in the abstract.)

3 Walk me through a GenAI project you built.
Strong answer

Leads with the problem and specific user, not a list of technologies. Explains the approach and why that approach was chosen over alternatives. Names one specific hard trade-off or decision made during building — the kind of thing a build-day checkpoint justification captures in the moment, rather than a vague after-the-fact summary. States actual success criteria and whether they were met, with real numbers.

Red flags

"I used LangChain, a vector database, and Claude" with no problem statement or reasoning; no mention of how they know it worked; can't name a single hard decision or trade-off, which suggests limited hands-on ownership of the actual build.

Follow-up

"What would you do differently if you rebuilt this from scratch today?" (Tests genuine reflection on the build, not a rehearsed pitch.)

4 An eval report shows accuracy dropped from 90% to 60% over the past month, with no code changes. Walk me through how you'd diagnose this.
Strong answer

Don't guess at a fix immediately — investigate the specific failing examples first to find the pattern. Consider drift explicitly: has the input distribution changed, has an underlying model or document set changed. Check whether the golden dataset itself is still representative, or whether it needs updating. Propose a systematic diagnostic process, not a single guess. This is exactly why Checkpoint 3 on a build day requires an actual eval pass rather than "did it run" — running without measuring is how a silent drop like this goes unnoticed for a month.

Red flags

Proposing a fix (e.g., "just retrain it" or "switch models") before investigating the actual failure pattern; not mentioning drift as a possible cause; treating this as necessarily a code bug.

Follow-up

"You find the drop is concentrated entirely in one specific question category. What does that tell you, and what would you check next?" (Points toward a narrower, more targeted root-cause investigation rather than a system-wide fix.)

5 A VP says: "Let's put AI on customer support, it's a mess right now." List at least three meaningfully different projects this could mean, and the one question you'd ask first.
Strong answer

Names at least three distinct possible projects (e.g., drafting reply suggestions, auto-routing tickets, predicting escalation risk, summarizing long threads) with different data and eval requirements. Correctly classifies at least one as predictive vs. generative. The clarifying question surfaces which specific pain point (speed, accuracy, agent workload) the VP actually means — because you can't pick a thin, buildable MVP slice until the actual target problem is pinned down.

Red flags

Only identifying one possible project, or treating "AI for support" as a single well-defined task; asking a generic clarifying question ("what do you want?") instead of one targeted at surfacing the ambiguity.

Follow-up

"The VP says 'all of the above, whatever's fastest.' How do you push back on scoping this into one project first?" (Tests whether they can hold the line on scoping discipline even against pressure to do everything at once.)

Strategy Tips

  • Interviewers often hand you a live scenario ("you have a week, go") rather than ask you to define MVP — practice giving a concrete time allocation, not a definition.
  • The follow-up question is usually the real test: "you're behind schedule, what do you cut?" A strong candidate cuts scope, not the evaluation step.
  • Naming a specific trade-off or decision from a real build is a reliable signal of hands-on ownership — vague technology lists are not.
