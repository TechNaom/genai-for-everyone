Mock Technical Interviews

A GenAI interview isn't one format — it's usually a mix of three, and each one rewards a different kind of preparation. Today you practice all three, using the vocabulary and judgment you've built across this entire program.

On this page 1. Format 1: "Walk me through a project you built" 2. Format 2: System design 3. Format 3: Live problem-solving 4. Giving and receiving feedback well
Answering real GenAI interview questions well isn't about having memorized the right vocabulary — it's about demonstrating the same judgment you've been building since Week 1. Today's session walks through the three formats you're most likely to see — "walk me through your project," system design, and live problem-solving — and what separates a strong answer from a weak one in each. It closes with the skill that makes mock interviews actually useful: giving feedback that's specific enough for someone to act on.
Part 1 of 4

1. Format 1: "Walk me through a project you built"

This is where your capstone (and your Session 7.1 proposal discipline) pays off directly. A strong answer follows a clear shape:

The four elements of a strong answer The problem and user (not "I built a RAG thing"); the approach and why that approach over alternatives; one specific hard decision or trade-off you made; and how you know it works — your actual success criteria, not "it seemed to work."

Interviewers are listening for judgment, not just that you can list technologies you used. "I used LangChain and a vector database" tells an interviewer nothing about why those were the right choices or what problem was actually being solved — describing the problem and a specific reasoned decision (why you chose that chunking strategy, for instance) demonstrates the judgment they're actually screening for.

Quick recap
  • A strong project answer names the problem/user, the approach and why, one hard trade-off, and real success criteria.
  • Listing a tech stack with no reasoning behind it is a weak answer, no matter how sophisticated the stack sounds.
Part 2 of 4

2. Format 2: System design ("design a GenAI feature for X")

These questions are rarely testing whether you know a specific API. They're testing whether you reach for the right questions before designing:

Ask these before describing any architecture What's the actual user and problem? Does this need retrieval, or is it a pure prompting problem (Session 3.1's "RAG or not?")? What's the eval plan — how would you know if this design is working (Week 5)? What breaks at scale, and what's the cost model (Week 6)?

A candidate who immediately starts describing an architecture without first asking these questions is optimizing for the wrong signal — the interviewer wants to see scoping instincts as much as technical knowledge.

Quick recap
  • System design questions test whether you ask the right questions first, not whether you know API syntax.
  • The four things to reach for: the real user/problem, RAG-or-not, an eval plan, and a cost/scale story.
Part 3 of 4

3. Format 3: Live problem-solving

"Here's a broken prompt/pipeline, fix it" or "here's an eval showing 60% accuracy, diagnose why." This tests the debugging instincts from Sessions 2.1–2.3 and 3.5 directly.

What this is actually testing Reading an example and its failure output for the pattern (formatting issue? missing context? wrong retrieval? ambiguous instructions?) rather than guessing at random fixes. Diagnosing the actual failure mode first is what separates a real debugging answer from a lucky guess.
Quick recap
  • Live-debugging questions test pattern recognition in failures, not trial-and-error fixing.
  • Read the specific symptom carefully before proposing any fix.
Part 4 of 4

4. Giving and receiving feedback well

A mock interview is only useful if the feedback afterward is specific. "That was good" teaches nothing.

Specific, actionable feedback "You jumped straight to describing your architecture before asking who the user was — that's the first thing a real interviewer would want to hear" is feedback someone can actually act on. When you're the interviewer in a mock session, hold yourself to that same standard.
Quick recap
  • Vague praise or criticism ("that was good/bad") gives the other person nothing to work with.
  • Good feedback names the specific moment and what should have happened instead.
In the Real World

Two candidates both built a similar RAG project. Candidate A describes it as "I used LangChain and a vector database to build a chatbot." Candidate B describes it as "Support agents were spending 12 minutes per ticket searching docs manually; I built a retrieval system over the internal wiki, chose chunking by section rather than fixed-size because the wiki's structure made that more accurate, and validated it against a 15-question golden set before considering it done." Candidate B is describing judgment; Candidate A is describing a tech stack. Interviewers remember the difference.

Points to Remember

  • "Walk me through your project" rewards judgment and trade-offs, not a list of technologies used — lead with the problem and the why behind your decisions.
  • System design questions are testing whether you ask the right questions first (RAG or not, eval plan, cost/scale) before describing an architecture.
  • Live debugging questions test pattern recognition in failures — read the specific symptom before guessing at a fix.
  • Feedback is only useful if it's specific and actionable — practice giving feedback you could actually act on, not just "that was good."
