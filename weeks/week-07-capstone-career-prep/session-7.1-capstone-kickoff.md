# Session 7.1: Capstone Kickoff

**Week:** 7 (Capstone Career Prep)
**Format:** Live session + self-paced exercise + quiz

## Learning objective

Scope a real-world GenAI capstone project down to something buildable in the remaining program time, with clear success criteria you could actually defend in a hiring interview.

## Concept (shared by everyone)

Six weeks in, you have real building blocks: prompting, RAG, agents, evaluation/safety, and deployment engineering. The capstone isn't about learning something new — it's about combining what you already have into one project substantial enough to anchor a portfolio and an interview conversation. Most capstones fail not because the building was too hard, but because the *scoping* was wrong: too broad to finish, too shallow to be interesting, or missing a way to know if it worked.

### The scoping trap

"I'll build an AI assistant that helps with everything at my job" is not a scope — it's a wish. A good capstone scope answers four questions concretely:

1. **What specific problem, for what specific user?** Not "help small businesses" — "help a solo bookkeeper triage which client emails need same-day replies."
2. **What does it actually do, end to end?** Input → processing → output, stated plainly enough that a non-technical person could picture it.
3. **What GenAI techniques from this program does it use, and why those?** A capstone that's "just a chatbot" wastes six weeks of RAG, agents, and eval training — a strong capstone visibly uses at least 2-3 of them for a real reason, not to check boxes.
4. **How will you know if it worked?** A concrete success criterion (from Week 5) — not "it feels good," but "correctly triages 8/10 golden-dataset emails" or similar.

### Sizing it to the time you have

You have roughly 2-3 more working sessions before demo day. A realistic capstone in that window is closer to "Week 3.6's policy bot, but with a real document set and Week 5's eval report," than to "a fully autonomous multi-agent platform." Ambition matters, but a finished, well-evaluated small project beats an unfinished ambitious one in every interview conversation — nobody can assess a demo that doesn't run.

### The one-pager

A capstone proposal 1-pager forces the scoping discipline above onto paper before you write any code. It should be short enough to read in two minutes and specific enough that a mentor reviewing it could immediately spot an over-scoped idea. Sections: problem + user, solution sketch, techniques used (and why), success criteria, and what's explicitly out of scope (just as important as what's in).

## Pro path — extended challenge

Write two competing proposals for the same underlying problem — one narrow-and-finishable, one ambitious-and-risky — and make an explicit written case for which you'd actually choose to build, given the time remaining, using the same reasoning a real team would use in a project kickoff meeting (scope vs. time vs. risk).

## Real-world scenario

A new hire's manager says "build us an AI feature for the customer portal" with no further detail. The employees who succeed are the ones who come back within a day with a scoped proposal — problem, users, approach, success metric, explicit non-goals — not the ones who disappear for two weeks and return with an ambitious half-built demo nobody asked for in that shape.

## Key takeaways

- A capstone scope needs a specific problem, a specific user, an end-to-end description, and a concrete success criterion — vague scope is the #1 cause of capstones that don't finish.
- A strong capstone visibly uses multiple techniques from this program (RAG + eval, or agents + guardrails) — not just a wrapped chatbot.
- Size the project to the time actually remaining; a finished small project outperforms an unfinished big one in every interview conversation.
- Explicitly stating what's *out* of scope is as valuable as stating what's in — it's what keeps you from silently expanding scope mid-build.
